# Reading map: reconstructing the classical Reformed account of natural law and natural theology

Scratch working document, not for publication. **Purpose, reframed 2026-07-29 — read this before the strands below:** this is fundamentally a constructive reading project. The goal is reconstructing, from primary sources, what Reformed orthodoxy actually held about natural law's content, reach, and relationship to special revelation and Christ's mediatorial kingship — for the sake of a clear, positively-stated account, not primarily to build a case against anyone. Testing that reconstruction against Klinean two-kingdoms political theology (the gap identified against Myers/Belcher/McGraw — a sustained natural-law argument spanning both Testaments, pinned to 1646 text) is a real secondary purpose and a genuine pressure-test, not the reason for the reading. Each strand's "what's actually at stake" framing below still poses the question in terms of what Kline's "common kingdom" needs — keep that as the sharpening question, but write the actual micro-articles as positive reconstruction first, contemporary relevance second. This is a reading plan and set of open questions, not a drafted argument — the synthesis itself is being reasoned out by the project owner as he reads.

Four strands, as named in conversation. Titles marked **[CONFIRM]** are my best guess at what's meant and need a yes/no before anything gets built on them.

## Workflow

Read → micro-article → strand synthesis → full article. Micro-articles are one-per-source (or per chapter/lecture/quaestio) reading notes, written in `research/reading-notes/<strand>/`, using `research/reading-notes/_template.md`. They're where the actual reading and reasoning happens — summarize the source's own argument first, in your own words, before any use is made of it, then note where it supports or complicates the working hypothesis below. Once a strand has enough micro-articles to show a real pattern, pause and write a short synthesis note before moving to the next strand or graduating material into an actual `series-*` article (which then goes through the six-lane pipeline in CLAUDE.md). This keeps output visible at every stage rather than banking everything on one long reading project before anything gets written.

Suggested starting point: Barker's *Natural Theology* ch. 1 or Burgess's *Vindiciæ Legis* Lecture VI — both already transcribed on pro-rege.com, both short, and both let you test-drive the micro-article format before tackling heavier scholastic Latin-in-translation material (Junius, Aquinas).

## Strand 1 — Natural theology & the noetic effects of sin

What's actually at stake: does fallen reason retain enough access to natural revelation to ground a "common," religiously-neutral civil realm (which is what Kline/VanDrunen's natural law needs to do the work they ask of it), or is natural knowledge of God real-but-severely-compromised in a way that undercuts that neutrality claim?

- **Augustine** — *City of God* (esp. bks. 8, 11, 19 on natural knowledge of the true God among the philosophers, and the two cities); *Enchiridion*. Augustine grants real natural knowledge of God to the philosophers (Rom 1) but insists it terminates in idolatry/suppression apart from grace — the root of the whole "noetic effects" question.
- **Aquinas** — *ST* I, qq. 1–13 (esp. q.12 on how we know God, analogical predication) and — this is probably the more load-bearing text for the political strand below — *ST* I-II, qq. 90–97 (the treatise on law: eternal, natural, human, divine). Read him as a source Reformed orthodoxy **revised and reorganized, not simply inherited** — Tomes's own point (in "Grace Perfects Nature," below) is that Reformed theologians "did not receive every element of the medieval synthesis unchanged," reworking Aquinas's categories around faith, sanctification, vocation, and law rather than habit, merit, and sacramental causality. Junius and Turretin are constantly in conversation with him, but critically as often as positively — don't let this strand quietly assume a smooth Aquinas-to-Reformed continuity that the sources themselves don't support.
- **Matthew Barker**, *Natural Theology* (1674), ch. 1 — **already transcribed on pro-rege.com** (2025/11/06 post). Good bridge text: a Puritan contemporary of Burgess working the same Romans 1 material Augustine and Aquinas anchor.
- **Turretin** — *Institutes*, Topic I (on theology) — his qq. on the being/nature/content of natural theology and its limits since the fall; also wherever he treats natural law within his doctrine of the moral law (Topic XI, on the law of God, is the other place to check).
- **Anthony Burgess**, *Vindiciæ Legis*, Lecture VI — **already transcribed on pro-rege.com**, directly on the law of nature written on the heart, its two elements (direction + obligation), and its perpetual binding force even on justified believers. This is a hinge text: it's simultaneously your natural-law source *and* already tied into the project's covenant-of-works/grace material via Burgess's anti-antinomian argument.
- **Stephen Charnock**, *The Existence and Attributes of God* (1682) — **moved here 2026-07-28**; this and the Brown of Haddington entry below were originally (wrongly) filed under the Romans 1-3 exegetical subsection, but both are systematic/natural-theology treatises, not commentaries. The classic Puritan natural-theology treatment of God's existence and attributes as known from reason and creation — a heavier, more systematic companion to Barker.
- **John Brown of Haddington**, *A Compendious View of Natural and Revealed Religion* (1782, relevant sections) — moved here alongside Charnock for the same reason. A later (Associate/Secession-tradition) Scottish digest of the natural/revealed-religion distinction, same genre as Barker and Charnock, not a Romans commentary.
- **Matt Marino, "'Noetic Effects of Sin' Objections"** (reformedclassicalist.com) — read alongside the historical sources above, not just as background: this is a living voice already working this exact question. See the full contemporary-voices entry below for his fuller corpus.
- **Jonathan Tomes, "Grace Perfects Nature"** (postnuance.substack.com) — constructive statement of the nature/grace relationship (Alsted's "nature... is healed and perfected by grace, not abolished," Polanus on natural moral good surviving the fall in impaired form) — a contemporary companion to Aquinas and Barker above.
- **Richard Muller** — added 2026-07-29, after auditing the project's academic secondary-literature gaps. This is the load-bearing modern scholarship for the whole "Reformed orthodoxy revised Aquinas, didn't just inherit him" point already built into this strand — read these before drafting anything that leans on that claim:
  - "Duplex Cognitio Dei in the Theology of Early Reformed Orthodoxy," *Sixteenth Century Journal* 10.2 (1979): 51-61 — the foundational essay on the natural/revealed-theology distinction underlying Reformed natural theology; likely the single best starting point among Muller's essays for this strand.
  - "Reading Aquinas from a Reformed Perspective: A Review Essay," *Calvin Theological Journal* 53.2 (2018): 255-88 — a review of K. Scott Oliphint's *Thomas Aquinas* arguing for *closer* Reformed-Thomist continuity than Oliphint allows. Read this specifically against the working assumption above ("Reformed orthodoxy revised Aquinas") — Muller may complicate that assumption rather than confirm it, which is exactly the kind of friction this strand's open questions already ask you to watch for.
  - "Was It Really Viral? Natural Theology in the Early Modern Reformed Tradition," in *Crossing Traditions* (Brill, 2018), 507-31.
  - *Post-Reformation Reformed Dogmatics*, 2nd ed., vol. 1, *Prolegomena to Theology* (Baker Academic, 2003) — natural theology is treated within the prolegomena/principia material; exact chapter not independently confirmed against a printed table of contents, so locate it directly rather than assuming a specific section number.
  - Also wrote the introductory essay to the *Treatise on True Theology* translation already tracked in Strand 2 (Junius) — read that essay alongside Junius's own text, not separately.

### Exegetical grounding: Romans 1–3

The whole strand answers to this text — general revelation suppressed (Rom 1:18-32), the law written on Gentile hearts and conscience (Rom 2:14-15), and universal sinfulness closing every mouth before it opens onto grace (Rom 3:9-20). This is exegete-lane territory (see `.claude/agents/exegete.md`, updated 2026-07-27 to track Rom 1-3 alongside its existing anchors) as much as it is natural-theology reading. **This subsection is commentaries specifically** (verse-by-verse exegetical work on Romans) — Charnock and Brown of Haddington were moved up to the main source list above 2026-07-28, since they're systematic natural-theology treatises, not Romans commentaries, and don't belong in this list on genre grounds even though they cover related ground:

**Reorganized 2026-07-28 into tiers, after the list grew past what's actually useful to read in full.** Read the core set first; treat the rest as backup for a specific citation or gap, not as a reading queue.

**Core — each earns its place for a distinct reason, read these:**
- **John Calvin**, *Commentary on Romans* — the source everyone else here works with or against; the foundational Reformed exegesis of exactly Rom 1:19-20 and 2:14-15.
- **William Sclater**, *A Key to the Key of Scripture, or an Exposition with Notes upon the Romans chs. 1-3* (d. 1626) — the only source in this list scoped to exactly Romans 1-3, nothing diluting it with the rest of the epistle. Read this one early.
- **Peter Martyr Vermigli**, *A Most Learned and Fruitful Commentary upon the Epistle of St. Paul to the Romans* (d. 1562; London, 1568) — added 2026-07-28. Distinctive among the 16th-c. options for engaging Aristotelian/scholastic categories directly and technically, unlike Calvin's more pastoral-exegetical style — a genuine primary witness to how early Reformed theology actually handled the nature/grace, scholastic-reception question this strand already flags for Aquinas, on this specific text rather than in the abstract.
- **Charles Hodge**, *Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans* — read this specifically to cross-check it against Hodge's own separately-tracked "law of Moses as re-enactment of the covenant of works" position (Series IV) — the payoff is the cross-check, not the commentary in isolation.
- **John Murray**, *The Epistle to the Romans* (NICNT, Eerdmans, 1968) — same logic in the opposite direction: Murray's disagreement with Hodge on Mosaic-covenant/republication is already flagged in Series IV, so read his Romans 1-3 exegesis to see whether that systematic disagreement actually shows up here or the two converge on this specific text.
- **David Dickson**, *The Epistle of Paul to the Romans, Analytically Expounded* (London, 1659) — read to cross-check against Tomes's citation of Dickson elsewhere (Strand 2, against VanDrunen's spatial two-kingdoms reading) — does Dickson's own Romans exegesis sit consonantly with how Tomes uses him, or is Tomes drawing on a different, non-Romans strand of Dickson's work?

**Secondary — pick one or two if you want another witness, not all of them:**
- **Matthew Poole**, *Annotations upon the Holy Bible* — a fast annotation/cross-reference tool rather than a deep exposition, useful for a quick check rather than a full read; note *Synopsis Criticorum* (Latin) and the English *Annotations* are different Poole works — confirm which is meant/available before citing.
- **John Brown of Wamphray** and **John Brown of Edinburgh** — kept as a pair specifically *if* you want to do the two-centuries-apart comparison exercise in the open questions below; if that comparison isn't a priority, reading just one of the two (Edinburgh's is the more highly regarded — Spurgeon called it "among the first of the first class") is enough.
- **Robert Haldane** — largely redundant with Brown of Edinburgh (same era, same tradition, often read as a pair) — skip unless a second 19th-c. Scottish witness is specifically wanted.
- **Matthew Henry** — devotional/homiletical rather than analytical; more useful for Series V's pastoral register than this strand's argument, so treat it as register-practice reading, not core natural-law source material.

**Narrow/specialized — situational, likely lower priority than the core set:**
- **Elnathan Parr**, "A Short View of the Epistle to the Romans 1:1–2:2" (in *The Works*, 4th ed., 1651, pp. 1-42) — short and likely thinner than Sclater's dedicated treatment of the same three chapters; a second witness to the same territory Sclater already covers, not a must-read on its own.
- **Thomas Wilson**, *A Commentary upon the most Divine Epistle of St. Paul to the Romans* (1614), 1,260 pp. — confirmed real, but that's a lot of pages to extract three chapters' worth of material; low efficiency unless doing exhaustive citation-hunting later.
- **James Morison**, *A Critical Exposition of the Third Chapter of Romans* (1866) — narrower still, Rom 3 only; useful only for a deep dive on that chapter specifically.
- **D. Martyn Lloyd-Jones**, *The Plight of Man and the Power of God* (1941) — modern, accessible, Romans 1-3 specific, but more preaching than historical-theological witness — a good pastoral-voice supplement, not core reading for the argument itself.
- Also real but not detailed further: Andrew Willet's *A Six-fold Commentary upon Romans* (1611) and Charles Ferme's *A Logical Analysis of Romans* (Scottish, d. 1617, published 1850).

**Open questions to read with:**
- Do Charnock, Barker, and the Romans commentators here (esp. Hodge and Calvin) treat Rom 2:14-15's "law written on the heart" as functionally available for actual civil/political use, or do they keep it within a more restricted pastoral/evangelistic use (i.e., proving universal guilt, not grounding a civil order)? That distinction is exactly where this strand's stakes live.
- Wamphray (17th c.) and Edinburgh (19th c.) sit two centuries apart on the same epistle — does either show more confidence than the other in natural law's civil applicability, and if so, does that track any broader shift in the tradition (e.g., toward or away from establishmentarianism) worth naming in Series IV?

## Strand 2 — Scholastic politics of natural law

What's actually at stake: whether the Reformed political tradition's own natural law was ever construed as an independent, religiously neutral sphere insulated from Christ's mediatorial government — or whether it was always located *within* a providential/covenantal frame that a "common kingdom" reading anachronizes.

- **Calvin** — *Institutes* IV.20 (civil government) — the foundational Reformed locus; note his own qualification that natural law's precepts, though real, need the moral law's clarification for fallen consciences (Inst. II.8.1, on the use of the Decalogue as a "mirror").
- **Junius** — Franciscus Junius, *The Mosaic Polity* (*De Politiae Mosis Observatione*, 1593; ET Todd M. Rester, CLP Academic, 2015) — **confirmed as primary**. This is the direct scholastic-era treatment of how Israel's judicial law relates to natural law and general equity, i.e., the primary-source background to WCF 19.4's "general equity" clause that the whole 2K/theonomy/general-equity debate turns on. Also very likely relevant: Junius's *A Treatise on True Theology* (*De Vera Theologia*, 1594; ET David C. Noe, Reformation Heritage Books, 2014) — this is where Junius develops the archetypal/ectypal theology distinction and treats natural vs. supernatural theology directly, i.e., a systematic locus with real natural-theology content, which may be the "systematic with pieces of this in it" you were recalling. **[CONFIRM]** before treating this as settled — flagging as the likely match, not yet verified against your memory of it.
- **Seung-Joo Lee**, *The Orders of Nature and Grace: Thomistic Concepts in the Moral Thought of Franciscus Junius (1545-1602)* (Brill, 2024) — added 2026-07-29, the first English monograph on Junius's theology in over 40 years and specifically on his Thomistic moral-theology sourcing. Read alongside Junius's primary texts, not instead of them — this is now the load-bearing secondary source for this strand's Junius material.
- **David VanDrunen's academic-register monograph** — worth distinguishing explicitly from the popular books already engaged elsewhere in this project: *Natural Law and the Two Kingdoms: A Study in the Development of Reformed Social Thought* (Eerdmans, 2010) is a serious historical monograph, published simultaneously with but distinct from his popular *Living in God's Two Kingdoms* (Crossway, 2010). This project has so far mostly engaged critiques *of* VanDrunen (Garris, Marino, Tomes) rather than his own academic-register primary case directly — worth reading this one firsthand before the strand's argument is considered complete, alongside his *Divine Covenants and Moral Order* (Eerdmans, 2014, already flagged in the manifesto as "primary text not yet read in full").
- **Althusius** — Johannes Althusius, *Politica* (1603; ET Frederick S. Carney, Liberty Fund, abridged; full new translation by Jeffrey Veenstra, 2020s). Covenantal/federal political theory grounded in natural law and consent via "symbiotic" associations — a major independent (non-Israelite-typology-dependent) Reformed natural-law political theory worth having as a comparison point.
- **Turretin** — wherever he treats the civil magistrate and natural law within his broader ethics/law material (cross-reference with Strand 1 above).
- **Gillespie** — George Gillespie, *Aaron's Rod Blossoming* (already in the project's bibliography) — the civil/ecclesiastical distinction and the Sanhedrin question; check specifically what role, if any, natural law plays in his argument versus purely positive Mosaic institution.
- **Bannerman** — *Church of Christ* (already in the project's bibliography) — parallel material on the church's spiritual, non-civil power.
- **Jonathan Tomes, "The Twofold Kingdom of Christ"** (postnuance.substack.com) — a contemporary voice already running this strand's exact question: he argues Christ's kingship is singular but *modally* differentiated (essential/mediatorial by title, purpose, and mode), not *spatially* divided into a common-civil and a redemptive-ecclesiastical kingdom the way he reads VanDrunen as doing — citing David Dickson directly against VanDrunen's spatial reading. Also independently treats the 1646-vs-1788 American revision as substantive, not a clarification — this project's own confessional discipline exactly. Full notes: `research/reading-notes/contemporary-kline-critics/tomes-twofold-kingdom-and-good-works.md`. Read this alongside Junius/Althusius/Gillespie, not just as a contemporary check afterward — it's making a historical-continuity argument of the same kind this strand is.

**Open questions to read with:**
- Junius distinguishes natural, common (gentile), and proper (peculiarly Israelite) judicial law — does that three-fold distinction actually support Kline/VanDrunen's common-kingdom/redemptive-kingdom split, or does it cut against it by keeping all three grounded in the same single moral order under God, rather than two separate "kingdoms" with two separate warrants?
- Does Althusius's natural law political theory ever get *religiously neutral* in Kline's sense, or is it consent-and-covenant-structured in a way that's already thicker than "common" natural law needs to be?
- Tomes reads VanDrunen's two kingdoms as a *spatial* misreading of what should be a *modal* distinction (title/purpose/mode, not territory). Do Junius's and Althusius's own categories actually support reading the historical tradition's distinction as modal rather than spatial — or is "modal vs. spatial" itself a frame Tomes is importing from Christology (the essential/mediatorial kingdom language) that these political sources don't independently supply? Worth testing whether Tomes and this strand are really making the same argument or only sound like they are.
- Given Tomes has already made a version of this strand's central claim in essay form, what does the fuller primary-source treatment (Calvin through Gillespie) need to establish that his piece doesn't — depth of citation, the Owen/Ball/Petto-era material he doesn't touch, or the confessional-citation rigor this project insists on throughout, not just at one flagged locus? Name the gap so this strand has a job distinct from restating him.

## Strand 3 — American Covenanter political theology

What's actually at stake: this is the sharpest possible contrast case to "common kingdom" theology — a Reformed political theology built explicitly on Christ's own mediatorial dominion over the nations, not a natural-law sphere insulated from his special kingship.

- **William Symington**, *Messiah the Prince: or, The Mediatorial Dominion of Jesus Christ* (1839). High-confidence title/date. The central primary text for this strand — Christ's headship over civil society grounded directly in his mediatorial office, not merely his office as Creator/providential ruler common to all.
- **Alexander McLeod** — **[CONFIRM]** exact title; my best recollection is *Messiah, Governor of the Nations of the Earth* (published from an 1803 sermon series), but verify before citing. McLeod (American RPCNA/Covenanter minister, Reformed Presbytery of New York, prominent anti-slavery preacher) is the American extension of the Scottish Covenanter mediatorial-kingship tradition.
- Possible further reading once these two are done: later American Covenanter systematics (J. M. Willson, David Steele) if you want the tradition's own internal development, not just its founding statements — flagging as a further-research item, not yet verified as existing/relevant.
- Cross-reference: Tomes's "The Twofold Kingdom of Christ" (Strand 2, above) bears directly here too — his modal essential/mediatorial distinction is a contemporary version of exactly what Symington and McLeod ground civil obligation in.
- **Secondary-literature gap, confirmed 2026-07-29 — this is the weakest-attested strand in the whole project.** No dissertation, monograph, or peer-reviewed article analyzing Symington's or McLeod's mediatorial-kingship *doctrine* specifically (as opposed to as a data point in broader political/social history) turned up in a real search pass. Closest adjacent academic scholarship: Joseph S. Moore, *Founding Sins: How a Group of Antislavery Radicals Fought to Put Christ into the Constitution* (Oxford UP, 2016) — political/social history of Covenanter abolitionism and church-state dissent, not doctrinal analysis; and Robert Emery, "Church and State in the Early Republic: The Covenanters' Radical Critique," *Journal of Law and Religion* 25.2 (2009): 487-501. Roy Blackwood and Michael LeFebvre, *William Symington: Penman of the Scottish Covenanters* (Reformation Heritage, 2009) exists but is a confessional/devotional publisher biography, not academic-press scholarship — don't cite it as filling this gap. **This means a careful, original doctrinal treatment of Symington and McLeod, brought to bear on Kline's common-kingdom theology, would be a genuinely novel contribution** — not a synthesis of existing arguments but actual new analysis filling a real, confirmed hole in the literature. Worth keeping in view as this strand is read, rather than assuming secondary scholarship will do any of the interpretive work here.

**Open questions to read with:**
- Symington and McLeod ground civil obligation in Christ's *mediatorial* kingship specifically (not merely his kingship as eternal Son/Creator) — does this actually require a covenant-theology commitment (i.e., does it only work if the nations are, in some sense, under the covenant of grace's reach, not merely natural law) in a way that would need explicit coordination with the manifesto's covenant-of-grace/administration argument, or can it stand on natural law alone?
- Where, if anywhere, do Symington or McLeod interact with (or anticipate) anything like a common/redemptive kingdom distinction, even to reject it? Or is the concept simply foreign to their whole framework?
- Tomes's modal essential/mediatorial distinction (Strand 2) is explicitly Christological — does it actually match how Symington and McLeod ground civil obligation, or do the Covenanters root it more directly in Christ's mediatorial office itself, without the essential/mediatorial modal apparatus Tomes borrows from elsewhere? If the two don't line up cleanly, that matters before treating Tomes as simply a contemporary restatement of the Covenanter position rather than a distinct (if convergent) argument.

## Strand 4 — Contemporary interaction: Van Til

What's actually at stake: Van Til's critique of natural theology's claimed neutrality is a live tension with *both* (a) the Reformed-classicalist retrieval project in Strand 1 and (b) Kline's own "common kingdom," since Kline's common-grace/common-kingdom theology was itself formed partly in a Van Tilian environment at Westminster.

- **Van Til** — *Introduction to Systematic Theology* (esp. the sections on natural theology and the noetic effects of sin) and *Common Grace and the Gospel* — his argument that "neutral" natural theology/natural law, considered in abstraction from one's fundamental religious commitment (regenerate vs. unregenerate presupposition), is an illusion; there is no religiously neutral common ground, even in the civil/natural realm.
- **Matt Marino, "Common Grace Conflated," "Van Til's Epistemology," "Kant and Van Til"** (reformedclassicalist.com) — a contemporary voice reading Van Til's own role in the 1920s Kuyper/Hoeksema common-grace debate directly, arguing Van Til made the discussion "apologetical rather than theological" and thereby muddied it. Read alongside the primary Van Til material, not as a secondary gloss — Marino has independently done real work on exactly this strand's central figure. See the Strand 1 contemporary-voices entry for his fuller corpus.
- Historical-genealogical question worth having in view (Series IV territory more than the manifesto): Kline was Van Til's colleague/successor context at Westminster: does Kline's "common kingdom" actually honor Van Til's anti-neutrality argument, or does it quietly reintroduce the neutral common ground Van Til spent his career denying? This is a real, documented tension some critics (e.g., some of the Van Tilian voices at reformedforum.org/Tipton, per the earlier research) already press — worth reading them once the primary Van Til material is in hand, rather than starting from secondary polemics.

**Open questions to read with:**
- If Van Til is right that there's no religiously neutral common ground, does that sink the "common kingdom" project outright, or only its *epistemological* framing (leaving room for a "common kingdom" that's admittedly not neutral but still distinct from the redemptive kingdom on other grounds)?
- Is there a way to hold Van Til's anti-neutrality point together with Strand 1's Augustine/Aquinas/Turretin material (real-but-impaired natural knowledge), or are they actually in tension with each other too, not just with Kline? (Keep the Aquinas nuance from Strand 1 in view here too — don't let this strand's use of "natural knowledge, real but impaired" quietly smuggle back in a more Thomistic-friendly picture than the Reformed sources themselves support.)
- Marino argues Van Til's own framing of the 1920s common-grace debate as primarily "apologetical rather than theological" was itself part of what muddied the discussion. If Marino's right, does this project's own use of Van Til's anti-neutrality argument need to be recast in more directly theological terms to avoid repeating the same muddying move?
- Given Marino has already argued a version of this strand's point directly from Van Til's own history, what does reading Van Til's primary texts firsthand need to add or correct in Marino's account, rather than simply confirm it?

## How the four strands might connect — a hypothesis to test, not a conclusion

One possible shape (yours to confirm, revise, or discard as you read): Kline/VanDrunen's "common kingdom" needs natural law to be (a) epistemically accessible in a religiously neutral way across regenerate/unregenerate alike, and (b) politically independent of Christ's mediatorial, redemptive kingship — a separate warrant for civil order. If Strand 1 (Augustine/Aquinas/Turretin on the noetic effects of sin) denies (a), and Strands 2–3 (the Reformed political tradition from Junius/Althusius through the American Covenanters) show natural law's civil authority was never actually separated from Christ's own government of the nations, then "common kingdom" theology isn't a small corollary of republication — it rests on a picture of natural law's neutrality and independence the Reformed tradition itself, on its own historical terms, didn't hold. Van Til's critique (Strand 4) would then be less an outside contemporary check and more a formal statement of the same problem the older material already demonstrates historically.

This is a real thesis-shape, but it's a hypothesis — the actual reading may complicate or overturn parts of it (e.g., Aquinas's or Junius's texts may turn out to support more "neutrality" than this expects). Flag anywhere the sources push back on it rather than smoothing over the friction.

Two things to keep in view while testing it, given where the reading and the audit have landed: first, don't let the hypothesis's reliance on Aquinas as a source for Strand 1 quietly assume Reformed orthodoxy simply continued him — the Reformed reworking of his categories (per Tomes's own point) is itself part of what this strand needs to document, not a detail to smooth past. Second, Marino and Tomes are independently testing pieces of this same hypothesis from a contemporary angle and, on the finds so far, are landing close to where it predicts — which is evidence worth taking seriously, but per the manifesto's revised framing it raises the bar rather than lowering it: the strands need to establish something their essays don't, not simply arrive at the same place by a longer road.
