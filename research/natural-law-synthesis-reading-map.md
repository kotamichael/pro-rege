# Reading map: reconstructing the classical Reformed account of natural law and natural theology

Scratch working document, not for publication. **Purpose, reframed 2026-07-29 — read this before the strands below:** this is fundamentally a constructive reading project. The goal is reconstructing, from primary sources, what Reformed orthodoxy actually held about natural law's content, reach, and relationship to special revelation and Christ's mediatorial kingship — for the sake of a clear, positively-stated account, not primarily to build a case against anyone. Testing that reconstruction against Klinean two-kingdoms political theology (the gap identified against Myers/Belcher/McGraw — a sustained natural-law argument spanning both Testaments, pinned to 1646 text) is a real secondary purpose and a genuine pressure-test, not the reason for the reading. Each strand's "what's actually at stake" framing below still poses the question in terms of what Kline's "common kingdom" needs — keep that as the sharpening question, but write the actual reading notes as positive reconstruction first, contemporary relevance second. This is a reading plan and set of open questions, not a drafted argument — the synthesis itself is being reasoned out by the project owner as he reads.

Four strands, as named in conversation. Titles marked **[CONFIRM]** are my best guess at what's meant and need a yes/no before anything gets built on them.

## Workflow

Read → reading note → strand synthesis → full article. Reading notes are one-per-source (or per chapter/lecture/quaestio), written in `research/reading-notes/<strand>/`, using `research/reading-notes/_template.md`. They're where the actual reading and reasoning happens — summarize the source's own argument first, in your own words, before any use is made of it, then note where it supports or complicates the working hypothesis below. They're a structured reading log (summary, quotes, hypothesis check, open questions, confidence ledger), not a small version of the finished article itself — that distinction matters, since the actual connected prose only comes later. Once a strand has enough reading notes to show a real pattern, pause and write a short synthesis note before moving to the next strand or graduating material into an actual `series-*` article (which then goes through the six-lane pipeline in CLAUDE.md). This keeps output visible at every stage rather than banking everything on one long reading project before anything gets written.

Suggested starting point: Barker's *Natural Theology* ch. 1 or Burgess's *Vindiciæ Legis* Lecture VI — both already transcribed on pro-rege.com, both short, and both let you test-drive the reading-note format before tackling heavier scholastic Latin-in-translation material (Junius, Aquinas).

## Strand 1 — Natural theology & the noetic effects of sin

What's actually at stake — **reworded 2026-07-29, see "How the four strands might connect" below for the full distinction**: not whether fallen reason retains real access to natural revelation (it does, universally, in believer and unbeliever alike — this strand isn't contesting that), but whether that universally-operative natural law is ever *religiously neutral* in its own ground of authority, the way Kline/VanDrunen's "common kingdom" needs it to be. Read every source below for that specific distinction — real-and-universal vs. autonomous-and-neutral — not for a generic "how much can reason know" question.

- **Augustine** — *City of God* (esp. bks. 8, 11, 19 on natural knowledge of the true God among the philosophers, and the two cities); *Enchiridion*. Augustine grants real natural knowledge of God to the philosophers (Rom 1) but insists it terminates in idolatry/suppression apart from grace — the root of the whole "noetic effects" question.
- **Aquinas** — *ST* I, qq. 1–13 (esp. q.12 on how we know God, analogical predication) and — this is probably the more load-bearing text for the political strand below — *ST* I-II, qq. 90–97 (the treatise on law: eternal, natural, human, divine). Read him as a source Reformed orthodoxy **revised and reorganized, not simply inherited** — Tomes's own point (in "Grace Perfects Nature," below) is that Reformed theologians "did not receive every element of the medieval synthesis unchanged," reworking Aquinas's categories around faith, sanctification, vocation, and law rather than habit, merit, and sacramental causality. Junius and Turretin are constantly in conversation with him, but critically as often as positively — don't let this strand quietly assume a smooth Aquinas-to-Reformed continuity that the sources themselves don't support.
- **Matthew Barker**, *Natural Theology* (1674), ch. 1 — **already transcribed on pro-rege.com** (2025/11/06 post). Good bridge text: a Puritan contemporary of Burgess working the same Romans 1 material Augustine and Aquinas anchor.
- **Turretin** — *Institutes*, Topic I (on theology) — his qq. on the being/nature/content of natural theology and its limits since the fall; also wherever he treats natural law within his doctrine of the moral law (Topic XI, on the law of God, is the other place to check).
- **Anthony Burgess**, *Vindiciæ Legis*, Lecture VI — **already transcribed on pro-rege.com**, directly on the law of nature written on the heart, its two elements (direction + obligation), and its perpetual binding force even on justified believers. This is a hinge text: it's simultaneously your natural-law source *and* already tied into the project's covenant-of-works/grace material via Burgess's anti-antinomian argument.
- **Stephen Charnock**, *The Existence and Attributes of God* (1682) — **moved here 2026-07-28**; this and the Brown of Haddington entry below were originally (wrongly) filed under the Romans 1-3 exegetical subsection, but both are systematic/natural-theology treatises, not commentaries. The classic Puritan natural-theology treatment of God's existence and attributes as known from reason and creation — a heavier, more systematic companion to Barker.
- **John Brown of Haddington**, *A Compendious View of Natural and Revealed Religion* (1782, relevant sections) — moved here alongside Charnock for the same reason. A later (Associate/Secession-tradition) Scottish digest of the natural/revealed-religion distinction, same genre as Barker and Charnock, not a Romans commentary.
- **Matt Marino, "'Noetic Effects of Sin' Objections"** (reformedclassicalist.com) — read alongside the historical sources above, not just as background: this is a living voice already working this exact question. See the full contemporary-voices entry below for his fuller corpus.
- **Jonathan Tomes, "Grace Perfects Nature"** (postnuance.substack.com) — constructive statement of the nature/grace relationship (Alsted's "nature... is healed and perfected by grace, not abolished," Polanus on natural moral good surviving the fall in impaired form) — a contemporary companion to Aquinas and Barker above.
- **Richard Muller** — added 2026-07-29, after auditing the project's academic secondary-literature gaps. This is the load-bearing modern scholarship for the whole "Reformed orthodoxy revised Aquinas, didn't just inherit him" point already built into this strand — read these before drafting anything that leans on that claim:
  - "Duplex Cognitio Dei in the Theology of Early Reformed Orthodoxy," *Sixteenth Century Journal* 10.2 (1979): 51-61 — the foundational essay on the natural/revealed-theology distinction underlying Reformed natural theology; likely the single best starting point among Muller's essays for this strand.
  - "Reading Aquinas from a Reformed Perspective: A Review Essay," *Calvin Theological Journal* 53.2 (2018): 255-88 — a review of K. ### Exegetical grounding: Romans 1–3

The whole strand answers to this text — general revelation suppressed (Rom 1:18-32), the law written on Gentile hearts and conscience (Rom 2:14-15), and universal sinfulness closing every mouth before it opens onto grace (Rom 3:9-20). This is exegete-lane territory (see `.claude/agents/exegete.md`, updated 2026-07-27 to track Rom 1-3 alongside its existing anchors) as much as it is natural-theology reading. **This subsection is commentaries specifically** (verse-by-verse exegetical work on Romans) — Charnock and Brown of Haddington were moved up to the main source list above 2026-07-28, since they're systematic natural-theology treatises, not Romans commentaries, and don't belong in this list on genre grounds even though they cover related ground:

**Reorganized 2026-07-28 into tiers, after the list grew past what's actually useful to read in full.** Read the core set first; treat the rest as backup for a specific citation or gap, not as a reading queue.

**Core — each earns its place for a distinct reason, read these:**
- **John Calvin**, *Commentary on Romans* — the source everyone else here works with or against; the foundational Reformed exegesis of exactly Rom 1:19-20 and 2:14-15.
- **William Sclater**, *A Key to the Key of Scripture, or an Exposition with Notes upon the Romans chs. 1-3* (d. 1626) — the only source in this list scoped to exactly Romans 1-3, nothing diluting it with the rest of the epistle. Read this one early.
- **Peter Martyr Vermigli**, *A Most Learned and Fruitful Commentary upon the Epistle of St. Paul to the Romans* (d. 1562; London, 1568) — added 2026-07-28. Distinctive among the 16th-c. options for engaging Aristotelian/scholastic categories directly and technically, unlike Calvin's more pastoral-exegetical style — a genuine primary witness to how early Reformed theology actually handled the nature/grace, scholastic-reception question this strand already flags for Aquinas, on this specific text rather than in the abstract.
- **Charles Hodge**, *Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans* — read this specifically to cross-check it against Hodge's own separately-tracked "law of Moses as re-enactment of the covenant of works" position (Series IV) — the payoff is the cross-check, not the commentary in isolation.

- **David Dickson**, *The Epistle of Paul to the Romans, Analytically Expounded* (London, 1659) — read to cross-check against Tomes's citation of Dickson elsewhere (Strand 2, against VanDrunen's spatial two-kingdoms reading) — does Dickson's own Romans exegesis sit consonantly with how Tomes uses him, or is Tomes drawing on a different, non-Romans strand of Dickson's work?

- **John Brown of Wamphray** and **John Brown of Edinburgh** — kept as a pair specifically *if* you want to do the two-centuries-apart comparison exercise in the open questions below; if that comparison isn't a priority, reading just one of the two (Edinburgh's is the more highly regarded — Spurgeon called it "among the first of the first class") is enough.

## Strand 2 — Scholastic politics of natural law

What's actually at stake: whether the Reformed political tradition's own natural law was ever construed as an independent, religiously neutral sphere insulated from Christ's mediatorial government — or whether it was always located *within* a providential/covenantal frame that a "common kingdom" reading anachronizes.

- **Calvin** — *Institutes* IV.20 (civil government) — the foundational Reformed locus; note his own qualification that natural law's precepts, though real, need the moral law's clarification for fallen consciences (Inst. II.8.1, on the use of the Decalogue as a "mirror").
- **Junius** — Franciscus Junius, *The Mosaic Polity* (*De Politiae Mosis Observatione*, 1593; ET Todd M. Rester, CLP Academic, 2015) — **confirmed as primary**. This is the direct scholastic-era treatment of how Israel's judicial law relates to natural law and general equity, i.e., the primary-source background to WCF 19.4's "general equity" clause that the whole 2K/theonomy/general-equity debate turns on. Also very likely relevant: Junius's *A Treatise on True Theology* (*De Vera Theologia*, 1594; ET David C. Noe, Reformation Heritage Books, 2014) — this is where Junius develops the archetypal/ectypal theology distinction and treats natural vs. supernatural theology directly, i.e., a systematic locus with real natural-theology content, which may be the "systematic with pieces of this in it" you were recalling. **[CONFIRM]** before treating this as settled — flagging as the likely match, not yet verified against your memory of it.
- **Seung-Joo Lee**, *The Orders of Nature and Grace: Thomistic Concepts in the Moral Thought of Franciscus Junius (1545-1602)* (Brill, 2024) — added 2026-07-29, the first English monograph on Junius's theology in over 40 years and specifically on his Thomistic moral-theology sourcing. Read alongside Junius's primary texts, not instead of them — this is now the load-bearing secondary source for this strand's Junius material.
- **David VanDrunen's academic-register monograph** — worth distinguishing explicitly from the popular books already engaged elsewhere in this project: *Natural Law and the Two Kingdoms: A Study in the Development of Reformed Social Thought* (Eerdmans, 2010) is a serious historical monograph, published simultaneously with but distinct from his popular *Living in God's Two Kingdoms* (Crossway, 2010). This project has so far mostly engaged critiques *of* VanDrunen (Garris, Marino, Tomes) rather than his own academic-register primary case directly — worth reading this one firsthand before the strand's argument is considered complete, alongside his *Divine Covenants and Moral Order* (Eerdmans, 2014, already flagged in the manifesto as "primary text not yet read in full").
- **Althusius** — Johannes Althusius, *Politica* (1603; ET Frederick S. Carney, Liberty Fund, abridged; full new translation by Jeffrey Veenstra, 2020s). Covenantal/federal political theory grounded in natural law and consent via "symbiotic" associations — a major independent (non-Israelite-typology-dependent) Reformed natural-law political theory worth having as a comparison point.
- **Turretin** — wherever he treats the civil magistrate and natural law within his broader ethics/law material (cross-reference with Strand 1 above).
- **Gillespie** — George Gillespie, *Aaron's Rod Blossoming* (already in the project's bibliography) — the civil/ecclesiastical distinction and the Sanhedrin question; check specifically what role, if any, natural law plays in his argument versus purely positive Mosaic institution.
- **Bannerman** — *Church of Christ* (already in the project's bibliography) — parallel material on the church's spiritual, non-civil power.
- **Jonathan Tomes, "The Twofold Kingdom of Christ"** (postnuance.substack.com) — a contemporary voice already running this strand's exact question: he argues Christ's kingship is singular but *modally* differentiated (essential/mediatorial by title, purpose, and mode), not *spatially* divided into a common-civil and a redemptive-ecclesiastical kingdom the way he reads VanDrunen as doing — citing David Dickson directly against VanDrunen's spatial reading. Also independently treats the 1646-vs-1788 American revision as substantive, not a clarification — this project's own confessional discipline exactly. Full notes: `research/reading-notes/contemporary-kline-critics/tomes-twofold-kingdom-and-good-works.md`. Read this alongside Junius/Althusius/Gillespie, not just as a contemporary check afterward — it's making a historical-continuity argument of the same kind this strand is.

**Open questions to read with:**
- Junius distinguishes natural, common (gentile), and proper (peculiarly Israelite) judicial law — does that three-fold distinction actually support Kline/VanDrunen's common-kingdom/redemptive-kingdom split, or does it cut against it by keeping all three grounded in the same single moral order under God, rather than two separate "kingdoms" with two separate warrants?

## Strand 3 — Christ's civil kingship: the mediatorial-dominion tradition and its internal fork

**Renamed and substantially reworked 2026-07-29.** Was "American Covenanter political theology," built around only Symington and McLeod — the two names the project owner happened to have at home, not a researched survey. Broadened at his request beyond RPCNA Covenanters specifically to the wider Scottish/American Presbyterian tradition (Free Church establishment-principle, Secession-church voices like Brown of Haddington), and — more importantly — a real internal fork within that wider tradition turned up in the research that needs to shape this whole strand's framing, not just its bibliography.

**The fork, and this is the actual finding to build around:** the Reformed tradition itself split on whether Christ's *mediatorial* kingship (as opposed to his general divine/essential kingship as eternal Son) extends over civil government, or is confined to the church.

**Working answer, 2026-07-29 (project owner's own judgment, pending primary-source confirmation):** this fork is narrower than it first looks, and doesn't actually complicate the project's core argument against Kline the way it seemed to. Gillespie's own track record (*Aaron's Rod Blossoming*, *Wholesome Severity Reconciled with Christian Liberty*) is hard establishment-principle, not proto-pluralism — his fight with the Erastians was over *who* holds spiritual power and *by what right*, never over whether the magistrate has real, substantive religious duties. So Gib's "church only" position, read in continuity with Gillespie, almost certainly still binds the magistrate to the first table of the law under Christ's general/essential kingship — it just declines to call that a *mediatorial* office specifically. If that holds up against the primary texts, **both forks agree magistrates are bound to the first table and civil order is not religiously neutral** — which is the actual point at stake against Kline. What's left is a real but narrower, internal Reformed question: which Christological office (mediatorial or essential/general) is the better-warranted ground for that shared conclusion — not whether Kline's neutral "common kingdom" is right (both forks already say no to that).
- **"Church only" view** — associated with **George Gillespie** (already tracked in Strand 2, *Aaron's Rod Blossoming* — worth rereading him specifically for whether *he himself* holds this, rather than assuming it from how later Seceders used him) and explicitly worked out by **Adam Gib** (1714-88), leader of the Antiburgher Secession, in ***The Present Truth: A Display of the Secession Testimony*** (2 vols., 1774) — vol. 2, Appendix 2, §4, "Christ's Mediatorial Kingdom & Common Benefits" (pp. 299-302), explicitly denies civil government derives authority from Christ's mediatorial office, grounding it instead in his general divine kingship. Vol. 1, Step 5, Part 2 (pp. 289-345) works the civil-government question from Rutherford's *Lex, Rex* Q.29 and Thomas Boston.
- **"Extends to civil government" view** — the Covenanter/Cameronian line, its classic 19th-c. systematic statement being **Symington's *Messiah the Prince*** (1839, unchanged from before).
- **CONFIRMED historiographical anchor for this whole fork**: David McKay, "From Popery to Principle: Covenanters and the Kingship of Christ," ch. 5 in Anthony T. Selvaggio, ed., *The Faith Once Delivered: Essays in Honor of Dr. Wayne R. Spear* (Reformation Heritage/P&R, 2007) — argues the "mediatorial kingdom over nations" doctrine was **not** the position of the *early* Scottish Covenanters; it developed later and became read back into the tradition's self-understanding "with little apparent awareness that it was not the doctrine of the early Covenanters." This is a real, citable, peer-reviewed-adjacent essay, not a blog claim — read it early, since it reframes everything else in this strand from "a settled tradition" to "a documented development." ### The Clark-Van Til controversy: analogical vs. univocal knowledge, and whether Van Til departs from historic orthodoxy

**Added 2026-07-29**, following up on a real historical question raised in conversation: does Van Til's insistence that human knowledge of God is *only ever analogical* — coinciding with God's own knowledge at no single point — represent a genuine departure from the classical Reformed archetypal/ectypal theology tradition (God's own perfect self-knowledge vs. the finite but genuinely true creaturely reflection of it), already tracked in this project via Junius? This bears directly on the strand's own natural-law question, not just general apologetic method: if Van Til's epistemology is applied consistently, does it threaten confidence that natural law's content can be *known clearly and truly* (Burgess: "does not nature condemn lying...?"), not just that it *binds* universally? That's a different, additional axis from the theonomous-vs-autonomous distinction already worked out in the main hypothesis below.

**The historical controversy, CONFIRMED:** Gordon H. Clark was ordained in the OPC in August 1944; a formal "Complaint" (October 1944, signed by twelve office-bearers including Van Til) charged that Clark's view collapsed the Creator-creature distinction by allowing some propositions to be known univocally (identically) by God and man, the divine/human difference being merely quantitative, not qualitative. The Complaint's own language: human knowledge "can never be identified with the knowledge which the infinite and absolute Creator possesses of the same proposition" — coinciding with God's "at no single point." Clark wasn't formally unseated but left the OPC in 1948; Van Til's position became the dominant epistemological consensus in that circle afterward. Full documentary detail: Douglas J. Douma, *The Presbyterian Philosopher: The Authorized Biography of Gordon H. Clark* (Wipf & Stock, 2017).

**Real, peer-reviewed academic support for the "this is a departure" reading:**
- **Richard Muller, "Reading Aquinas from a Reformed Perspective: A Review Essay,"** *Calvin Theological Journal* 53.2 (2018): 255-87 (already tracked in Strand 1, now central here too) — reviewing K. Scott Oliphint's *Thomas Aquinas* (P&R, 2017), a Van Tilian reading of Aquinas's analogy-of-being doctrine as covertly rationalist/univocal. Muller's counter: Aquinas needed analogical predication *specifically because* he held the Creator-creature distinction firmly — analogy exists to protect divine transcendence, not compromise it. Verdict: Aquinas's assumptions "align with the Westminster Confession and mainstream Reformed orthodoxy," and it's Oliphint's Van Tilian antithesis that's the historical outlier. This is the sharpest, most direct academic case found on this whole question — obtain the actual CTJ pages before quoting verbatim (blocked from automated access; secondary summaries only so far).
- **Kevin DeYoung, response to Nathan D. Shannon,** *Westminster Theological Journal* 83.2 (2021), replying to Shannon, "Junius and Van Til on Natural Knowledge of God," *WTJ* 82.2 (2020): 279-300 — DeYoung argues directly that classical ectypal natural theology *is* genuinely true theology with real, non-arbitrary content, not merely analogical noise, "from Turretin through Old Princeton to Warfield" — in tension with Van Til's (and Shannon's) stronger formulation. This is the most direct existing academic engagement with the Junius archetypal/ectypal category versus Van Til specifically — read the whole exchange (Shannon's rejoinder too), not just DeYoung's side.
- **Richard Muller, "Kuyper and Bavinck on Natural Theology,"** *The Bavinck Review* 10 (2019): 5-35 — states Van Til represented a real divergence within the Dutch Reformed/neo-Calvinist tradition itself, "strenuously opposed to Bavinck's recourse to the older scholasticism in general and Aquinas in particular."

## How the four strands might connect — a hypothesis to test, not a conclusion

**Reworked 2026-07-29 — the earlier version of this hypothesis conflated two different claims under the single word "neutral," and the project owner caught it while reading Burgess.** Kline/VanDrunen's "common kingdom" actually needs natural law to do two distinct things, and they have to be kept separate:

- **(a1) Universal operation** — natural law must be really present and really binding in every conscience, regenerate and unregenerate alike, apart from special revelation. **This the Reformed tradition affirms without hesitation, and so does this project.** Burgess's own example: Cain was bound by, and guilty under, the law of nature "although there was not Moral Law as yet given" (Lecture VI). Rom 1:20 and 2:14-15 say the same. Nobody in this dispute — not this project, not Kline, not Van Til — denies (a1).
- **(a2) Autonomous/neutral ground of authority** — natural law must additionally be a free-standing moral order whose validity doesn't depend on, or point back to, the true God specifically, such that it can function as genuinely common ground between true religion, false religion, and no religion at all. **This the tradition denies, and this is the actual load-bearing claim Kline's "common kingdom" needs and doesn't have.** Per Burgess, natural law's bindingness is never located in bare human reason or conscience taken as an independent faculty — he explicitly rejects that as "very uncertain," varying between men "as it is with measures in diverse countries" — it binds only because conscience functions as "the vicegerent of God, or a command from him" (Lecture VI). Its authority is theonomous, not autonomous. Van Til's anti-neutrality argument (Strand 4) says the same thing in different vocabulary: universal confrontation with revelation is real and inescapable (that's (a1) again), but there is no religiously neutral common ground for *interpreting* it (the denial of (a2)) — because the thing being confronted with was never neutral to begin with. Turretin's already-flagged distinction is this same point a third way: natural theology's being real is not the same claim as its being sufficient to found a religiously neutral public order.

So the sharper form of the hypothesis: it was never that natural law fails to reach the unregenerate or is somehow inaccessible to them — that's a claim this project and Scripture both reject, and pressing it would concede ground unnecessarily. It's that natural law, however universally operative, was never capable on its own terms of being *religiously neutral* — because its very authority is borrowed, not owned. Kline's "common kingdom" needs (a2); the Reformed tradition's own sources, on their own terms, give him (a1) and refuse him (a2). Strands 2-3 (the Reformed political tradition, on whichever side of the Gillespie/Symington fork proves right — see Strand 3's working answer) add the political half of the same point: natural law's *civil* authority was never actually treated as independent of God's own government of the nations either, for the same theonomous-not-autonomous reason. Van Til (Strand 4) is then less an outside contemporary check on the older material and more a formal restatement of a distinction (a1 vs. a2) the tradition already drew without needing his vocabulary to do it.

This is a real thesis-shape, but it's a hypothesis — the actual reading may complicate or overturn parts of it (e.g., Aquinas's or Junius's texts may turn out to support more "neutrality" than this expects). Flag anywhere the sources push back on it rather than smoothing over the friction.

Two things to keep in view while testing it, given where the reading and the audit have landed: first, don't let the hypothesis's reliance on Aquinas as a source for Strand 1 quietly assume Reformed orthodoxy simply continued him — the Reformed reworking of his categories (per Tomes's own point) is itself part of what this strand needs to document, not a detail to smooth past. Second, Marino and Tomes are independently testing pieces of this same hypothesis from a contemporary angle and, on the finds so far, are landing close to where it predicts — which is evidence worth taking seriously, but per the manifesto's revised framing it raises the bar rather than lowering it: the strands need to establish something their essays don't, not simply arrive at the same place by a longer road.
