# Noahic Covenant / Kline-VanDrunen Cheat Sheet

Prep for listening to a Klinean lecture on the Noahic covenant. Drawn from: Garris's "Seven Arguments" chapter, Marino's video/written corpus, and four independent Genesis commentaries (Murphy, Leupold, Poole, Calvin) read specifically against this question. Full sourcing in `research/reading-notes/contemporary-kline-critics/garris-critique-of-modern-r2k.md`, `research/reading-notes/contemporary-kline-critics/marino-*.md`, and `research/reading-notes/noahic-covenant-commentaries/`.

**Tone note going in:** this is a live intramural Reformed debate, not a fight — Kline and VanDrunen are serious, careful theologians, and your pastor is presenting their position fairly. The goal of these questions is genuine clarification, not a gotcha. Several of the sharpest points below (esp. §3 and §7) are honest complications *for* the critique of Kline, not just ammunition against him — know where your own case is weaker, not just where it's strong.

## The claim being tested

R2K's whole "common kingdom" (civil government, natural law, religiously neutral common ground) rests on reading Genesis 6-9 as **two separate covenants**: a personal, redemptive one with Noah alone (Gen. 6:18), and a second, universal, *non*-redemptive one with all creation (Gen. 9:8-17). If that division doesn't hold, the "common kingdom" has no textual foundation — Garris calls this division "the sine qua non of R2K theology."

---

## 1. Is it actually two covenants? (The load-bearing question — press here first)

**The claim:** Gen. 6:18 and Gen. 9 are two different covenants — one redemptive/personal, one common/universal.

**What's against it:**
- Four independent commentators spanning 1554-1942 (Calvin, Murphy, Leupold, and Poole's *Synopsis*, which itself surveys still more historic interpreters) all read this as **one covenant**, promised in ch. 6 and confirmed/fulfilled in ch. 9 — not two.
- **Even Kline's own words cut against the division.** He states Genesis 9's covenant was designed "to subserve the divine purpose and program of redemptive covenant" (*Kingdom Prologue*, 261) — his own stated purpose for the "common grace covenant" already concedes it serves redemption. He also calls the Noahic community "a family, not a nation to which a kingdom-treaty might appropriately be directed" — undercutting his own followers' use of it as the charter for a civil/political "common kingdom."
- The Hebrew verb at 9:9 (*heqim*) is read by both Murphy and Poole as "confirm/ratify a covenant already entered into," not "make a new one" — matching the argument Gentry/Wellum/Garris make. (Honest complication: Leupold reads the same verb differently at 6:18, as forward-looking institution rather than confirmation of something prior — so don't present the philology as more settled than it is.)
- Noah's sacrifice (8:20) is explicitly the *ground* of God's resolve in 8:21, and can't be neatly walled off into a separate, earlier "covenant" the way VanDrunen tries to.

**Ask:** *"If Genesis 9 is a genuinely separate, non-redemptive covenant, why does Kline himself describe its purpose as serving the redemptive covenant? And does the text ever call it a second covenant, or does it consistently use language of confirming/establishing what was already spoken to Noah?"*

## 2. Even granting natural law's full content, does civil jurisdiction really narrow to the Second Table?

**The claim:** Civil government's competence is limited to Second-Table (man-to-man) matters; the Noahic covenant establishes this as a "bare minimalist ethic."

**What's against it:** VanDrunen's own definition of natural law already includes the First Table — he grants (in his own words) that Reformed orthodoxy "equated the content of the natural law with the moral law... as summarized in the Decalogue." So the narrowing to Second-Table-only isn't coming from natural law's content — it's smuggled in downstream, through a specific reading of the Noahic covenant's silence on worship. But Genesis 9 doesn't even legislate most Second Table crimes either (adultery, theft, the murder/manslaughter distinction) — if silence there doesn't mean "forbidden," silence on worship shouldn't either.

**Ask:** *"If natural law's content already includes true worship of God — which VanDrunen himself grants — on what basis does the Noahic covenant's silence about worship translate into a prohibition on the magistrate acting on it?"*

## 3. Is Noah's sacrifice "mere consecration," severable from the covenant? (Know this one is genuinely mixed — don't overclaim)

**The claim (VanDrunen):** the sacrifice is consecratory, not expiatory, and doesn't make the Noahic covenant redemptive.

**Honest state of the evidence:** this is NOT a clean win. Murphy reads it as expiatory/substitutionary. Leupold reads thanksgiving *and* propitiation blended. Poole's gloss emphasizes gratitude/worship-acceptance with no expiation language. **Calvin explicitly denies the sacrifice had any meritorious or appeasing efficacy in itself** — "nothing can be more absurd than to suppose that God should have been appeased by the filthy smoke of entrails." So don't press "the tradition says it's expiatory" as settled fact — a Klinean could reasonably answer with Calvin.

**But here's the actual point that survives:** even Calvin, who denies the sacrifice's ritual efficacy, still treats the whole passage as saturated with covenantal/faith content — he uses Genesis 9's covenant structure elsewhere to **refute the Anabaptists on infant covenant-inclusion**, treating it as sharing the same intergenerational covenant-of-grace logic as everywhere else in his theology. That's very hard to square with reading Genesis 9 as religiously thin or "common" in VanDrunen's sense, regardless of how the sacrifice question itself lands.

**Ask:** *"Whatever we conclude about the sacrifice's exact efficacy, does the covenant that follows it read as religiously neutral common ground — or does even someone like Calvin, who denies the sacrifice's own efficacy, still treat this whole passage as operating on covenant-of-grace logic?"*

## 4. Does Genesis 9:1-7 really institute only a "minimalist" civil ethic?

**The claim:** the Noahic covenant's civil content is deliberately minimal — just enough to preserve basic human society, nothing more.

**What's against it:** Both Murphy and Leupold read Gen. 9:1-7 as instituting rich civil authority grounded directly in the image of God — not a bare survival provision. Murphy: the death penalty reflects "the law of requital, which is the universal principle of justice," and civil rule is to be exercised "in the Lord." Leupold, following Luther: "by this word government is instituted... power over the lesser things is naturally included" once power over life itself is granted. That's a foundational charter for civil authority generally, not a narrowly fenced minimum.

**Ask:** *"If Genesis 9:6 institutes civil government as such — as both Murphy and Leupold read it — what textual warrant limits that institution's scope to only punishing murder, rather than treating it as the ground for civil authority's broader competence?"*

## 5. What does "common" actually mean here — objective or subjective?

**The claim:** the Noahic covenant establishes a "common" kingdom — common to believer and unbeliever, a shared field distinct from the church.

**The sharper question (Marino's contribution):** "common" is doing double duty. Defined *objectively* — by what God himself has bound all people to, a real field with real, God-given boundaries — "common" is a legitimate, historic word (English "commonwealth" itself comes from a word for shared *good*, not neutral ground). Defined *subjectively* — by whatever people happen to agree they share, discovered by consensus rather than given by God — "common" has no principled floor and drifts downward indefinitely. 1 Samuel 8 is the paradigm case: Israel's demand for a king "like the nations" was a rejection of God's own kingship, and once "common" gets defined by the people's own voice rather than God's institution, there's no natural stopping point.

**Ask:** *"When we call this a 'common' kingdom, do we mean common because God himself fenced it that way, or common because it's whatever the culture happens to agree on? Those aren't the same claim — which one is actually doing the work in this argument?"*

## 6. Does the essential/mediatorial kingship distinction really wall off civil life from Christ's mediatorial reign?

**The claim:** Christ's *mediatorial* kingship is confined to the church; civil government answers only to his general/essential divine kingship (hence "common," not specifically Christian).

**What's against it:** The historic Reformed *duplex regnum Christi* doctrine (per Beeke's peer-reviewed monograph, cited independently by both Garris and Jonathan Tomes) distinguished Christ's essential and mediatorial kingdoms **by mode of governance, not by scope or territory** — the two "comprise one kingdom that is universal in scope." The mediatorial/essential distinction was never meant to fence civil life outside Christ's mediatorial authority; it described *how* he rules, not *where*.

**Ask:** *"Was the historic essential/mediatorial distinction ever meant to divide up territory — civil life under one office, church life under the other — or was it about the mode of Christ's governance within one kingdom that's universal in scope either way?"*

## 7. The single best "steelman the critique against itself" question

If the lecture is careful and well-argued (likely, if your pastor is doing this seriously), the strongest move isn't a rebuttal at all — it's asking him to locate his own view relative to the internal tension already documented: **Kline's own texts don't fully support the uses later R2K writers make of his division.** That's not a hostile question; it's asking him to do exactly the kind of careful distinguishing this project also insists on (Owen isn't Kline; Kline isn't VanDrunen).

**Ask:** *"Does Kline's own statement that the Noahic covenant was designed to 'subserve... redemptive covenant' sit comfortably with the sharp common/redemptive divide his later followers built on it — or is there daylight between what Kline himself wrote and how it's since been used?"*

---

## If you only remember one thing

The whole R2K "common kingdom" stands or falls on Genesis 6-9 being **two** covenants. Every commentator checked so far — across four centuries, none of them writing with Kline in view — reads it as **one**. That's the hinge. Everything else (jurisdiction, the sacrifice, "common," mediatorial kingship) is downstream of that single exegetical question.
