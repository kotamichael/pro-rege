# James G. Murphy, *Commentary on Genesis*, on Gen. 6:9-22, 8:15-22, and 9:1-17

**Full citation:** James G. Murphy, *A Critical and Exegetical Commentary on the Book of Genesis, with a New Translation* (Andover: Warren F. Draper, 1866; earlier ed. Belfast, 1863). Murphy (1808-1896) was Professor of Hebrew at Queen's College, Belfast. Full text: [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/criticalexegetic00murp) (OCR'd, public domain).

**Read on:** 2026-08-06, as the first of a planned sequence of pre-Kline/pre-R2K commentaries on Genesis 6-9, checked specifically against Zachary Garris's "Seven Arguments against the Klinean/R2K Division of the Noahic Covenant" (`research/reading-notes/contemporary-kline-critics/garris-critique-of-modern-r2k.md`) — prompted by the project owner's pastor teaching a session on the Noahic covenant from a Klinean perspective.

## What the source actually argues

Murphy treats Gen. 6:9-22 ("The Ark," §XXVI), 8:15-22 ("The Ark Left," §XXVII), and 9:1-17 (capital-punishment section + "The Covenant with Noah," §XXIX) as one continuous narrative and theological unit, not as containing two separate covenants. His treatment has three load-bearing moves:

1. **On Gen. 6:18** ("I will establish my covenant with thee"): Murphy locates this as the *textual* "first appearance of a covenant between God and man on the face of Scripture," but insists the *substance* is older — "a covenant from the very beginning, though the name do not appear," first "a covenant of works, in regard to man," now (post-fall) "a covenant of grace to the penitent sinner." His gloss on "my covenant": "the word *my* points to its original establishment with Adam. My primeval covenant, which I am resolved not to abandon." He treats Noah's covenant as a continuation of a single covenant of grace already operative since Eden, not a new, freestanding arrangement.

2. **On Gen. 8:20-22** (Noah's sacrifice and God's resolve not to curse the ground again): Murphy reads the sacrifice as substitutionary and expiatory, not merely consecratory — "he acknowledges his guilt, the claim of the offended law upon his life, and the mercy of the Lord in accepting a substitute to satisfy this claim for the returning penitent... a well-understood foreshadowing of the coming victim for human guilt." He explicitly ties God's resolve (v. 21) to the sacrifice as its ground: "the reason for the promise of escape from the fear of a deluge for the future is the sacrifice of Noah, **the priest and representative of the race**, with which the Lord is well pleased."

3. **On Gen. 9:8-17** (the rainbow covenant, headed by Murphy "XXIX. The Covenant with Noah"): Murphy opens the section flatly: **"The covenant made with Noah (Gen. vi. 18) is now formally confirmed."** His note on v. 9 ("I establish my covenant"): "He not merely makes [it], but ratifies, his covenant with them. *My covenant.* The covenant which was before mentioned to Noah in the directions concerning the making of the ark, and which was really, though tacitly, formed with Adam in the garden." He explicitly calls the Gen. 9 covenant "clearly of grace," notes it "presupposes and supplements the covenant with man subsisting from the very beginning," and states "the meritorious condition of the covenant of grace is dimly shadowed forth in the burnt-offerings which Noah presented on coming out of the ark" — directly linking the Gen. 8:20 sacrifice into the Gen. 9 covenant's own grace-structure, not treating them as belonging to two different covenants.

4. **On Gen. 9:1-7** (dominion, capital punishment): Murphy grounds civil government and the death penalty in the image of God as such — man's capacity for "self-government in civil things," rule "in the Lord" and obedience "in the Lord," and "the law of requital, which is the universal principle of justice." This is a rich moral-governmental reading, not a bare survival-level "minimalist ethic."

## Key quotes

> "My covenant. The covenant which was before mentioned to Noah in the directions concerning the making of the ark, and which was really, though tacitly, formed with Adam in the garden." (on Gen. 9:9)

> "The covenant made with Noah (Gen. vi. 18) is now formally confirmed. The purpose conceived in the heart (Gen. viii. 21) now receives significant expression. Not only a new blessing is bestowed, but also a new covenant is formed with Noah." (opening of §XXIX, on Gen. 9:8-17 — note "a new covenant is formed" here refers to a fuller ratification/expression of the one covenant, not a second, different covenant; Murphy's own next sentence calls it a "confirmation," and his note on v. 9 explicitly denies it is a fresh, unrelated institution)

> "The reason for the promise of escape from the fear of a deluge for the future is the sacrifice of Noah, the priest and representative of the race, with which the Lord is well pleased." (on Gen. 8:21)

> "A covenant is a solemn compact, tacit or express, between two parties... Consequently, between God and man there was of necessity a covenant from the very beginning, though the name do not appear. At first it was a covenant of works, in regard to man; but now that works have failed, it can only be a covenant of grace to the penitent sinner." (on Gen. 6:18)

> "The function of self-government in civil things is now conferred upon man... He must rule in the Lord, and they must obey in the Lord." (on Gen. 9:1-7)

## Bearing on the project's hypothesis and the Kline/VanDrunen critique

This is a strong, independent, pre-critical (1863) witness against the Klinean/R2K division of Genesis 6-9 — independent in the fullest sense, since Murphy is writing seven decades before Kline and has no stake whatsoever in the modern R2K debate. Three direct points of contact with Garris's seven arguments:

- **Argument #1 (heqim berit as "confirm," not "initiate a new covenant")**: Murphy doesn't use the Hebrew technical vocabulary (*heqim* vs. *karat*) the way Gentry/Wellum/Garris do, but he independently reaches the same substantive conclusion by close reading: Gen. 9 "formally confirms" Gen. 6:18; the verb in 9:9 means God "not merely makes [it], but ratifies" a covenant already existing. This is real corroborating evidence for Garris's conclusion, from a different (exegetical-contextual rather than lexical-comparative) angle — but it doesn't independently verify the specific philological claim about *heqim*'s technical meaning, which is still the open, UNVERIFIED item flagged in the Garris reading note. Keep those two forms of evidence distinct when citing this.
- **Argument #7 (Noah's sacrifice is inseparable from the Gen. 9 covenant, and is expiatory not merely consecratory)**: Murphy is a direct, independent hit here. He reads the sacrifice as substitutionary/expiatory ("acknowledges his guilt... the mercy of the Lord in accepting a substitute") and explicitly makes it the *ground* of God's resolve in both 8:21 and, by extension, the confirmed covenant of chapter 9 — exactly the connection VanDrunen denies when he insists the sacrifice belongs only to a separate, "first" (allegedly redemptive) covenant of Gen. 6:18 and doesn't color the "second" (allegedly non-redemptive) covenant of Gen. 9.
- **The manifesto's own "one covenant of grace, two administrations" thesis**: Murphy's move to root Noah's covenant explicitly in a covenant "really, though tacitly, formed with Adam in the garden," and to describe Gen. 9 as confirming rather than replacing it, is a genuine, independent confirmation of this project's own central claim extended one step earlier than usual — not just Sinai as an administration of the one covenant of grace, but Noah's covenant too. Worth citing directly if Series II or a Noahic-covenant article wants a pre-Kline exegetical anchor for treating Noah's covenant as continuous with, not categorically separate from, the Abrahamic and later administrations.

One honest complication: Murphy's own language at the start of §XXIX ("a new covenant is formed with Noah") could, read out of context, sound like it supports a two-covenant reading. It doesn't — his very next clauses and his note on v. 9 make clear he means a fuller *expression/ratification* of the existing covenant, not a second, different one — but this is a place where a hostile or careless reading of Murphy could misquote him against his own actual position. Flag this if Murphy is ever quoted briefly rather than in context.

## Open questions raised

- Murphy's dating of the covenant's *substance* to Eden ("really, though tacitly, formed with Adam in the garden") raises a question for this project's own Series I terminology work: is Murphy conflating the covenant of works (with unfallen Adam) and the covenant of grace (with fallen Adam, Gen. 3:15) into one continuous "covenant" merely by extending the *word* backward, or is he making a more careful claim that the covenant of grace itself (not the covenant of works) was already "tacitly" present immediately upon the fall and is what continues through Noah? His own next sentence ("At first it was a covenant of works... but now... a covenant of grace") suggests the latter, more careful reading — but this is worth checking against how carefully this project's own Series I wants to use "covenant" language pre-Sinai.
- Does Murphy ever address the Gen. 6:18 vs. Gen. 9 "singular you" vs. "plural you"/offspring distinction that Kline and VanDrunen lean on as evidence of two different covenant-parties? Not found in the sections read — worth a targeted second pass if this specific argument needs a pre-Kline commentator's answer.
- Murphy's reading of Gen. 9:1-7 as establishing rich civil-governmental and moral content (not a bare "minimalist ethic") is a useful data point against VanDrunen's specific characterization of that passage — worth checking whether Calvin, Leupold, or Poole's *Synopsis* independently converge on this same "not minimalist" reading, or whether Murphy is unusually maximalist here relative to the wider tradition.
