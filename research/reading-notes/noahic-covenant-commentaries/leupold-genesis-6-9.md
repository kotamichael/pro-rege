## What the source actually argues

Leupold reads Gen. 6:18, 8:20-22, and 9:8-17 as one continuous covenant narrative — a promise given before the flood (6:18), motivated and grounded by a sacrifice after it (8:20-21), and formally carried into effect after that (9:9-17). He states this explicitly at 9:8-10: **"The covenant which was promised Gen. 6:18 before the Flood, here goes into effect."** This is agreement with Murphy's conclusion (no two covenants), but Leupold gets there by a **different and more technical philological route** than either Murphy or Garris — and that route surfaces a real complication worth taking seriously rather than smoothing over.

**On the Hebrew verb itself, Leupold's position is more nuanced than Garris's use of Gentry/Wellum.** At Gen. 6:18, Leupold notes the verb is *heqîm* ("to set up"), not *karath* (the usual verb for "cutting"/entering a covenant), but concludes *heqîm* here "must mean 'make' not 'keep,' **for the covenant is not yet made**" — i.e., at 6:18, prospective/future-oriented, not a confirmation of something already in force. Then at 9:9, he notes the *same* verb *heqîm* is used again, but here glosses it differently: "the discussion runs quite beside the point... this word reckons with the covenant as practically existent and concerns itself merely with 'carrying into effect' (BDB) its provisions." So on Leupold's own account, *heqîm* is doing two different jobs across the two texts — prospective promise at 6:18, fulfillment/execution at 9:9 — rather than "confirming a covenant already fully in force since even earlier" (which is closer to how Murphy and Garris's own citation of Gentry/Wellum frame it). The conclusion (one covenant, not two) is the same; the philological mechanics supporting it are meaningfully different, and Leupold's own reading of 6:18 doesn't actually support the claim that *heqîm* generally signals confirmation-of-a-prior-covenant — in that verse he explicitly denies the covenant is yet made at all.

**On Noah's sacrifice (8:20-21)**, Leupold takes a deliberately blended position against a one-sided reading in either direction: **"Taking only the expressions of v. 21, some regard the purpose of this sacrifice to be only propitiation... we find the ruling out of the idea of thanksgiving in connection with this sacrifice to be preposterous... The purposes of thanksgiving and of propitiation blend in this sacrifice."** This is a real, honest middle position between Murphy's fuller expiatory/substitutionary reading and VanDrunen's "consecration, not expiation" claim — Leupold affirms real propitiatory content (contra VanDrunen) but insists it's not exclusively that (a caution worth respecting rather than flattening toward whichever side needs it more).

**On Gen. 9:6 (image of God grounding capital punishment/civil authority)**, Leupold reads it, following Luther, as instituting civil government as such, not a narrow, minimal provision: **"By this word government is instituted, this basic institution for the welfare of man. For if man receives power over other men's lives under certain circumstances, then by virtue of having received power over the highest good that man has, power over the lesser things is naturally included."** This directly supports the "not a bare minimalist ethic" reading already found in Murphy, from an independent conservative commentator working seventy years before the R2K debate.

**On critical/documentary-hypothesis handling of this material**, Leupold explicitly rebuts source-critical rearrangement of the text (naming Procksch specifically for proposing to reverse the order of Gen. 9:1-7 and 9:8-17): "Such efforts confirm critics in the erroneous thoughts they read into the text." This confirms Leupold is engaging critical scholarship on this exact passage, though the specific instance found was about verse-order, not a direct rebuttal of a two-covenant JEDP-style division of 6:18 vs. ch. 9 — worth a further, more targeted look if that specific critical-scholarship angle is needed later.

## Key quotes

> "The covenant which was promised Gen. 6:18 before the Flood, here goes into effect." (on Gen. 9:9-10)

> "[At 6:18] The usual expression for entering upon a covenant (*karath*) is not here used but the verb *heqîm*, 'to set up'... which must mean 'make' not 'keep,' for the covenant is not yet made." (on Gen. 6:18)

> "[At 9:9] Here the verb used for the setting up of the covenant is not *karath*... but the Hifil *heqîm*... used... in the sense of 'keeping' or 'carrying out.' For when Gen. 6:18 promised a covenant as future, this word reckons with the covenant as practically existent and concerns itself merely with 'carrying into effect' its provisions." (on Gen. 9:9)

> "The purposes of thanksgiving and of propitiation blend in this sacrifice." (on Gen. 8:20-21)

> "By this word government is instituted, this basic institution for the welfare of man." (on Gen. 9:6)

## Bearing on the project's hypothesis and the Kline/VanDrunen critique

**Confirms the core conclusion (no two covenants) but complicates the specific philological argument for it.** This is a case where a source is more valuable for the friction it surfaces than for simple agreement. Garris's argument #1 (via Gentry/Wellum) leans on *heqîm* meaning "confirm a covenant already in force" as opposed to "cut"/initiate a new one — implying the word itself signals *continuity with something already established*. Leupold's own reading of *heqîm* at 6:18 cuts directly against that: there, he says, the same verb "must mean 'make' not 'keep,' for the covenant is not yet made" — i.e., Leupold reads *heqîm* at 6:18 as **prospective, future-oriented institution**, not confirmation of a prior covenant. He only gets to "carrying into effect a covenant already existent" at 9:9, and even there frames it as fulfilling a *promise* made at 6:18, not confirming a covenant that had already been fully "made" earlier (contrast Murphy, who traces the covenant's substance all the way back to Adam). This means: the "one covenant, not two" conclusion is well-supported across multiple independent readers (Murphy, Leupold, and per Garris, Belcher, Robertson, Fesko, Hamilton, Cassuto, Dumbrell, Williamson), but the specific lexical claim that *heqîm* itself always or typically signals confirmation-of-a-prior-covenant is not uniformly held even among unity-affirming commentators — Leupold's own analysis, taken on its own terms, would need *heqîm*'s meaning to flex between "make" (6:18) and "carry out" (9:9) rather than meaning "confirm" consistently in both places. **This is exactly the kind of friction the Garris reading note already flagged as an open question ("is this reading of Gentry/Wellum's lexical claim actually settled among Hebraists...") — Leupold is now positive evidence that it isn't uniformly settled, not just an unverified worry.**

**The sacrifice question is a genuine middle position, not a simple win for either side.** Leupold's "thanksgiving and propitiation blend" is a real complication for citing this material as flatly pro-expiation (contra VanDrunen) — it partially supports Garris/Murphy against VanDrunen's "consecration, not expiation" claim, but doesn't go as far as Murphy's fuller substitutionary language. Cite Leupold carefully here — as blended, not as unambiguously expiatory.

**The civil-government/image-of-God point (9:6) is a clean, independent confirmation** against VanDrunen's "bare minimalist ethic" reading of Gen. 9:1-7 — a second conservative commentator (after Murphy) reading that text as instituting rich governmental authority, not a narrow survival-level provision.

## Open questions raised

- Does Leupold ever address the Gen. 6:18 "singular you" vs. Gen. 9 "you and your seed" distinction directly (the argument some two-covenant advocates use)? Not found in the sections read — worth a targeted second pass.
- Given Leupold's own account requires *heqîm* to mean something different at 6:18 (prospective institution) than at 9:9 (carrying out a promise), is there a cleaner, more consistent philological case for covenant unity available elsewhere (perhaps in Poole's *Synopsis*, which surveys the full history of interpretation) — or is flexing the verb's sense across the two texts simply the correct reading, in which case Garris's own presentation (via Gentry/Wellum) may be overstating the lexical uniformity of the point? Worth deciding before this project leans on the *heqîm* argument as cleanly as Garris does.
- Leupold's rebuttal of Procksch's verse-reordering proposal shows he engages critical scholarship on this passage, but not (in what was read) the specific two-source (JE/P) explanation for why Gen. 6:18 and Gen. 9 both mention a covenant. Worth a further, more targeted search of Leupold's introduction/excursus material (not just the verse-by-verse comments) if the project wants a conservative rebuttal of the *source-critical* version of the "two covenants" claim specifically, as distinct from Kline's *theological* version of the same textual division.
