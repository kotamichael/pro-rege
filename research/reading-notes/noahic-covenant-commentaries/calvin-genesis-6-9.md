# John Calvin, *Commentary on Genesis*, on Gen. 6:18, 8:20-22, and 9:8-17

**Full citation:** John Calvin, *Commentary on Genesis*, vol. 1 (Baker, 1996 reprint of the Calvin Translation Society edition). Full text: [Christian Classics Ethereal Library](https://ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom01.html), plain-text edition (clean, not OCR).

**Read on:** 2026-08-06, fourth in the planned sequence of pre-Kline commentaries on Genesis 6-9 in this folder (after Murphy, Leupold, Poole). **Status: CONFIRMED** — read directly from clean CCEL text, no OCR or translation-confidence caveats needed here (unlike the Poole note).

## What the source actually argues

Calvin treats Gen. 6:18 and Gen. 9:8-17 as one continuous covenant unfolding across the narrative — he says so explicitly, with a direct forward cross-reference from the first passage to the second. But his treatment of the Gen. 8:20-21 sacrifice is the most surprising find in this whole sequence: Calvin explicitly **denies** the sacrifice itself had any expiatory or meritorious efficacy, redirecting the entire weight of God's acceptance onto Noah's faith and gratitude — the cleanest denial of an "expiation" reading among all four commentators read so far, and a real complication for using this material against VanDrunen's "consecration, not expiation" claim.

**On Gen. 6:18**, Calvin reads the covenant as centered on Noah's preservation through the flood ("the sum of this covenant... was, that Noah should be safe, although the whole world should perish"), but explicitly signals continuity forward: **"the covenant with him is confirmed, with this condition annexed, that his family shall be preserved for his sake; and also the brute animals, for the replenishing of the new world; concerning which I shall say more in the ninth chapter."** This is Calvin himself treating Gen. 9 as the fuller unfolding of the same covenant named at 6:18, not a second, different one — though, notably, he does not make the technical Hebrew-verb argument (*heqim* vs. *karath*) that Murphy and Poole both lean on; his case for continuity is narrative and theological, not lexical.

**On Gen. 9:8-11**, Calvin makes the covenant's scope and character explicit in a way none of the previous three commentators did as directly: **"It was not therefore a private covenant confirmed with one family only, but one which is common to all people, and which shall flourish in all ages to the end of the world."** He then uses this directly as a polemical weapon against the Anabaptists on an entirely different doctrinal question — infant inclusion in the covenant: **"Hence the ignorance of the Anabaptists may be refuted, who deny that the covenant of God is common to infants, because they are destitute of present faith... the fathers were... intermediate parties between God and their children, whose office it is to deliver to their children... the promise received from God."** This is a genuinely important, unexpected find: Calvin treats the Noahic covenant's intergenerational structure as parallel to, and evidence for, the same covenant-succession logic operative in the covenant of grace generally (the basis of infant baptism) — meaning he is not reading Genesis 9 as a lesser, "merely natural" or religiously thin arrangement at all, but giving it full covenantal, theological weight.

**On Gen. 8:20-21 (Noah's sacrifice)**, Calvin's reading runs in a different direction than Murphy's. He first affirms the sacrifice was divinely warranted, not Noah's own invention (Noah "undertook nothing without divine authority"), then turns sharply on the question of *efficacy*: **"nothing can be more absurd than to suppose that God should have been appeased by the filthy smoke of entrails, and of flesh... it is not even to be supposed, that the rite of sacrifice, in itself, was grateful to God as a meritorious act."** He relocates the entire weight of divine acceptance onto Noah's inner disposition: **"What else did Noah propose to himself than to acknowledge that he had received his own life... as the gift of God's mercy alone? This piety breathed a good and sweet odour before God."** The "sweet savor" is glossed as *odorem quietis* ("a savor of rest") — God's declaration that the offering was rightly made, not a claim that the offering itself accomplished anything before God.

## Bearing on the project's hypothesis and the Kline/VanDrunen critique

**On covenant unity**: a fourth independent confirmation, and arguably the theologically strongest one yet — not because of technical philology (Calvin doesn't make that argument), but because Calvin treats the Noahic covenant as sharing the *same covenantal logic* (intergenerational transmission, common to all ages, a real promise resting on God's own word) as the covenant of grace elsewhere — strong enough that he weaponizes it against a *completely different* controversy (Anabaptist rejection of infant covenant-inclusion). This is hard to square with VanDrunen's "common kingdom," religiously-thin reading of the same passage: if Calvin can use Genesis 9's covenant structure to argue for paedobaptism by analogy, he is plainly not treating it as a bare, non-redemptive, "common" arrangement standing apart from the covenant of grace's own logic.

**On the sacrifice question, this is a real complication, not a confirmation.** Across the four commentaries read so far: Murphy (expiatory/substitutionary) — Leupold (thanksgiving *and* propitiation, blended) — Poole (worship/gratitude accepted, no expiation language found) — and now Calvin, who goes further than Poole and *explicitly denies* the sacrifice had meritorious or appeasing efficacy in itself. This project should not claim "the classic Reformed tradition reads Noah's sacrifice as expiatory, against VanDrunen's mere-consecration reading" as a simple, uncontested historical fact — Calvin, arguably the single most authoritative Reformed voice among all four sources, is the strongest example yet against that specific claim. **This doesn't rescue VanDrunen's larger argument, though** — Calvin's own reason for denying efficacy-in-the-rite is thoroughly covenantal and faith-centered (Noah's faith, God's prior authorization, gratitude for God's mercy), not a "common," religiously-neutral reading of the passage. VanDrunen needs the *scene* to be religiously thin or common; Calvin's reading makes it more, not less, saturated with covenantal/faith content — he just locates that content in Noah's piety rather than in the blood of the sacrifice.

**Net effect on Garris's argument #7**: the specific claim that the sacrifice is "expiatory, not merely consecratory" (used against VanDrunen) needs to be either dropped or handled much more carefully — Calvin doesn't support it, and arguably would resist it as a matter of principle (denying meritorious efficacy to any Old Testament sacrifice considered as a bare rite, consistent with his broader sacramental theology). What survives, strongly, across all four commentators including Calvin, is that the sacrifice is *covenantally load-bearing* — the ground, in some real sense, of what follows — even if its exact theological mechanism (expiation vs. accepted-gratitude) is a genuine point of historic disagreement.

## Open questions raised

- Calvin's denial that the sacrifice was "in itself... a meritorious act" is consistent with his general sacramental theology (signs signify, they don't automatically confer) — is this a distinctively Calvinian move, or does it reflect a broader Reformed-vs-Lutheran/Roman difference on the *ex opere operato* question that's worth tracing more carefully before generalizing "the Reformed tradition" from Calvin alone?
- Calvin's Anabaptist-refutation move (Gen. 9's covenant structure supporting infant inclusion) is a genuinely interesting parallel argument this project hasn't used yet — is this citable more broadly for Series I/II's own covenant-of-grace continuity argument, independent of the Kline/VanDrunen question entirely?
- None of the four commentators read so far (Murphy, Leupold, Poole, Calvin) make the Gen. 6:18 "singular you" vs. Gen. 9 "you and your seed" argument that some two-covenant advocates lean on. Worth checking a commentator who takes the *opposing* (divided) position, if one can be found among older sources, rather than only reading commentators already inclined toward unity — this project's own reading map elsewhere flags the risk of only finding what it's looking for.
