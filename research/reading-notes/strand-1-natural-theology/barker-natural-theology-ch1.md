## Context assembled before reading (2026-07-30) — read Barker first, independently, before drawing comparisons to Burgess

**Who he was.** Matthew Barker (1619 – 25 March 1698), born Great Cransley, Northamptonshire; worked as a schoolmaster in Banbury before the Civil War, then became a London preacher. Unlike Burgess, Barker was an **Independent** (Congregationalist), not a Presbyterian Assembly divine — same broad Puritan moment, different ecclesiastical wing. Preached before the House of Commons Oct. 25, 1648. Ejected from St. Leonard's, Eastcheap, London, in 1662 (the same Great Ejection that took Burgess the same year, for the same refusal of the Act of Uniformity) — worth noting as a real point of contact between the two even though their church polities differed. Also contributed to Matthew Poole's *Annotations* on Thessalonians — a direct, confirmed link to Poole, already tracked in this project's Romans 1-3 commentary list.

**The text itself, per the pro-rege.com page's own framing:** Chapter I argues God's eternal power and divine nature are discernible through creation itself, without written revelation — directly exegeting Romans 1:20. His core moves: the mind reasons from visible effects to their invisible, infinite cause; God himself remains imperceptible to the senses, but his attributes manifest in what he's made; he distinguishes the initial creation of matter from the subsequent forming of creatures, arguing both reveal divine power and wisdom; and he concludes creation gives sufficient knowledge of God to leave all people "without excuse" (Rom 1:20's own language) for rejecting him.

**Why this is paired with Burgess as a starting point** (per the reading map's Workflow section): both are short, already-transcribed, and working the same Romans 1 material Augustine and Aquinas anchor at the heavier end of this strand — a good test of whether 17th-century Puritan divines land closer to Augustine's "real knowledge, but terminates in idolatry/suppression" caution, or strike a more confident note about natural theology's positive reach. Burgess's own lecture (already read) hedges hard on natural law's *bounds* being reliably measurable, while still holding its core content and obligation are real and binding. Read Barker with that specific comparison in mind — he's making a *natural theology* argument (knowledge of God's existence/attributes from creation) rather than Burgess's *natural law* argument (moral obligation written on the heart) — related but not identical questions, and worth keeping the distinction visible rather than treating them as the same claim from two authors.

**No secondary scholarship dedicated to Barker specifically has been chased down yet** — unlike Burgess (where Casselli's monograph and several journal articles exist), Barker appears to be a thinner-studied figure. If a real modern treatment turns up, add it here; don't assume its absence means it's not worth checking.

## Bearing on the project's hypothesis

**The project's working hypothesis, in short** (full version in `research/natural-law-synthesis-reading-map.md`, "How the four strands might connect"): Kline/VanDrunen's "common kingdom" needs natural law/theology to do two distinct things that have to be kept separate. **(a1) Universal operation**: real, present, and binding knowledge of God and his moral demands in every person, regenerate or not — the Reformed tradition affirms this without hesitation, and so does this project. **(a2) Autonomous/neutral ground of authority**: that this knowledge is additionally free-standing, its validity independent of the true God, such that it could function as genuinely neutral common ground between true religion, false religion, and no religion at all — this the tradition denies. Barker is Strand 1 material testing both: his whole argument depends on (a1) being true (creation really does convey knowledge of God to everyone, hence "without excuse"), but the interesting question is whether he treats that knowledge as *self-standing* — a "natural theology" that could, in principle, function apart from and neutrally toward the true God's own further self-revelation — or whether, like Burgess's "vicegerent of God" language, its very authority already presupposes and points back to God specifically, denying (a2) even while affirming (a1) strongly.
