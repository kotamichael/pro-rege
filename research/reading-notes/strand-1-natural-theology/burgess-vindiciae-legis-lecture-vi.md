## Context assembled before reading (2026-07-28) — read Burgess first, independently, before the secondary material below

**Who he was, and why this is insider testimony, not commentary.** Burgess was elected vicar of St. Lawrence Jewry (Guildhall, London) in January 1645 and delivered these thirty lectures *while sitting as a Westminster Assembly member* — specifically on the Assembly's committee on Antinomianism. His work there "likely influenced" WCF 19's language on the law's use "sweetly complying" with the gospel (Casselli's claim, below — verify independently before citing as settled). Ejected 1662 for refusing the Act of Uniformity. Publication date given as both 1646 and 1647 across sources — check the actual title page of whatever edition/transcription is being used rather than assume either.

**The occasion:** direct combat against the English Antinomian controversy — Robert Towne (suspended 1629), John Eaton, Tobias Crisp — who argued the law was wholly abrogated for believers. The danger Burgess was answering: Antinomians used the same grace-vocabulary as unimpeachably orthodox Puritans (Perkins, Sibbes), so a careless "law vs. gospel" antithesis wouldn't distinguish orthodoxy from error — he had to draw the line with real precision.

**Book's overall shape** (per Casselli, below): law given to Adam → law given to Moses → the proper relation of law and gospel.

**The specific theological move a secondary source (see below) says to watch for:** Burgess reportedly works with a *narrow* law-gospel distinction (law demands perfect obedience, gospel offers pardon, genuinely opposed in justification — continuous with Luther/Beza/Ursinus) and a *broad* one simultaneously (the whole Sinai administration, "with the preface and promises adjoined," considered broadly as itself "a Covenant of grace" — grounded in God being Israel's Father per Deut 6:4, forgiveness being provided for, and Moses/Christ as mediator). On this reading he lands closer to Ball's "pure administration" pole than to Owen's administrative-republication or Cameron/Bolton/Petto's subservient-covenant reading — **confirm this directly from the text itself, don't take the secondary source's word for it.**

**Quotable material already recovered (secondary-sourced, verify page numbers against the actual edition before citing):** "He that knoweth how to distinguish between Law and Gospel, let him give thanks to God" (A3); "the Jews experienced drops of grace, we, as Christians, have access to the fountain of grace"; law "directeth, commandeth and humbleth," gospel to "melt the heart into kindness."

**Secondary scholarship worth knowing exists:** Stephen J. Casselli, *Divine Rule Maintained: Anthony Burgess, Covenant Theology, and the Place of the Law in Reformed Scholasticism* (Reformation Heritage, 2015) — the dedicated modern monograph; also Casselli, "God the Lawgiver According to the Westminster Divine Burgess," *Unio Cum Christo* 4.1 (2018); David R. Como, *Blown by the Spirit* (Stanford, 2004), the standard history of the Antinomian underground; David Parnham, "Motions of Law and Grace," *WTJ* 70.1 (2008).

**Important caution, not just a footnote:** a two-part examination of this exact text ran on R. Scott Clark's Heidelblog in April 2025 ("To Obey Or Not To Obey..."). Clark is this project's single most substantial tracked *defender* of Kline's republication/2K theology (see `research/contemporary-interlocutors-map.md`) — his site has an institutional stake in how a Westminster-divine's law/gospel theology gets read, and the piece may (consciously or not) frame Burgess in ways favorable to a law-gospel-distinction-heavy, Escondido-adjacent reading. It's real, substantive secondary material worth having — but read Burgess's own text first and form an independent judgment before letting that framing set your expectations.

**Companion reading, not urgent:** Samuel Bolton, *The True Bounds of Christian Freedome* (1645) — same moment, same fight, but Bolton sits in the Cameron/Petto "subservient covenant" camp rather than wherever Burgess actually lands — a useful contrast once Burgess's own position is clear.

## Key quotes

> In the next place, there are two consequents of the Law which are ad bene esse, ‘that the Law may be better obeyed’; and this indeed turns the Law into a covenant, which is another notion upon it, as afterwards is to be shown. Now as for the sanction of the Law by way of a promise, that is a mere free thing; God, by reason of that dominion which he had over man, might have commanded his obedience, and yet never have made a promise of eternal life unto him. —[Pro Rege transcription](https://pro-rege.com/2025/12/12/how-is-this-law-said-to-be-written-in-their-hearts-burges/)

> The differences of its being in Adam and in us. This is necessary to observe; for it was perfectly implanted in Adam’s heart, but we have only some fragments, and a mere shadow of it left in us. —ibid

> Some bind [The Law of Nature] by reason in every man: but this is very uncertain, and one man’s reason is contrary to another’s, and one man’s conscience is larger than another’s; even as it is with measures in diverse countries, though they have the same name, as a bushel, etc. yet they are different in quantity, one is larger than another. —ibid

> Lastly, Others bound it by the will of God, declared and manifested to Noah in seven precepts, and afterwards to Moses in the Ten Commandments: but these extend the Law of Nature not only to first principles, but conclusions also deduced from thence. —ibid

> The obligation of it, when the Law of Nature does bind: and that is from God the author of it, God only is under no law. Every believer, though justified by Christ, is under the Moral Law of Moses, as also the law of Nature: but now this Law of Nature does not so properly bind, as it’s man’s reason or conscience, as that it is the vicegerent of God, or a command from him: and thus Cain by the Law of Nature found a tie upon him not to sin, and guilt because he did sin in murdering his brother, although there was not Moral Law as yet given. It is true, indeed, our Divines do well reprove the papists, for calling all that time from Adam to Moses, as state, or Law of Nature: and this the papists do, that therefore to offer sacrifice unto God may be proved from the Law of Nature; whereas those sacrifices being done in faith, had the word of God, otherwise we were bound still to offer lambs and kids to God, which they deny.

> Does not Nature condemn lying, cozening in your trades, lusts, and uncleanness?

## Bearing on the project's hypothesis

**The project's working hypothesis, in short** (full version in `research/natural-law-synthesis-reading-map.md`, "How the four strands might connect"): Kline/VanDrunen's "common kingdom" needs natural law to be (a) accessible in a religiously neutral way to believer and unbeliever alike, and (b) politically independent of Christ's own mediatorial kingship — a separate warrant for civil order answerable to something other than him. If natural law's post-fall reliability turns out to be more compromised than premise (a) requires, and if the Reformed political tradition never actually treated natural law's civil authority as separate from Christ's own government of the nations (premise (b)), then "common kingdom" theology rests on a picture of natural law the Reformed tradition itself didn't hold. This Burgess lecture is Strand 1 material specifically — it's testing premise (a): does his account of the law written on the heart (its direction and obligation) describe natural law as reliably clear in practice, or does it hedge its post-fall reliability in a way that would cut against Kline's "common kingdom" needing it to be straightforwardly neutral ground?

## Open questions raised

- Would be interested in more detail regarding common notions, the medium etc. 
- Want to know more about the distinction between speculative principles of natural law and practical. For instance, perhaps he means the same common notion can imply speculative import and practical: e.g. speculative: that there is a God; practical: That God should be worshipped. Seems the practical is somewhat practical in itself and vice versa for the practical.
- Burgess mentions almost in passing that only fragments of the Law of Nature as it was given to Adam remain in us. How extensive is the reduction/corruption in us post-fall? What remains? What is the nature of the corruption? In what way is it reduced, e.g. categorically, quantitatively?
- "Lastly, Others bound it by the will of God, declared and manifested to Noah in seven precepts, and afterwards to Moses in the Ten Commandments: but these extend the Law of Nature not only to first principles, but conclusions also deduced from thence." I'm not entirely sure what Burgess means by conclusions vs first principles here. I think he means these are too defined to rest at the level of common notions, we would agree they are more like summations of the moral law.
- In the quote above, regarding Cain having from nature a tie not to sin and guilt for having murdered, is Moral Law being used to refer to the Decalogue? Or just that there was not yet a divine command positively given yet not to murder (as there was given to Noah)? Seems that if the former, God had at least warned him that the fruit of his anger (implied to be murder) ought to be avoided. Maybe this fits the bill as a divine precept prohibiting murder?
