**Read on:** 7/29/26

## Context assembled before reading (2026-07-28) — read Burgess first, independently, before the secondary material below

**Who he was, and why this is insider testimony, not commentary.** Burgess was elected vicar of St. Lawrence Jewry (Guildhall, London) in January 1645 and delivered these thirty lectures *while sitting as a Westminster Assembly member* — specifically on the Assembly's committee on Antinomianism. His work there "likely influenced" WCF 19's language on the law's use "sweetly complying" with the gospel (Casselli's claim, below — verify independently before citing as settled). Ejected 1662 for refusing the Act of Uniformity. Publication date given as both 1646 and 1647 across sources — check the actual title page of whatever edition/transcription is being used rather than assume either.

**The occasion:** direct combat against the English Antinomian controversy — Robert Towne (suspended 1629), John Eaton, Tobias Crisp — who argued the law was wholly abrogated for believers. The danger Burgess was answering: Antinomians used the same grace-vocabulary as unimpeachably orthodox Puritans (Perkins, Sibbes), so a careless "law vs. gospel" antithesis wouldn't distinguish orthodoxy from error — he had to draw the line with real precision.

**Book's overall shape** (per Casselli, below): law given to Adam → law given to Moses → the proper relation of law and gospel.

**The specific theological move a secondary source (see below) says to watch for:** Burgess reportedly works with a *narrow* law-gospel distinction (law demands perfect obedience, gospel offers pardon, genuinely opposed in justification — continuous with Luther/Beza/Ursinus) and a *broad* one simultaneously (the whole Sinai administration, "with the preface and promises adjoined," considered broadly as itself "a Covenant of grace" — grounded in God being Israel's Father per Deut 6:4, forgiveness being provided for, and Moses/Christ as mediator). On this reading he lands closer to Ball's "pure administration" pole than to Owen's administrative-republication or Cameron/Bolton/Petto's subservient-covenant reading — **confirm this directly from the text itself, don't take the secondary source's word for it.**

**Quotable material already recovered (secondary-sourced, verify page numbers against the actual edition before citing):** "He that knoweth how to distinguish between Law and Gospel, let him give thanks to God" (A3); "the Jews experienced drops of grace, we, as Christians, have access to the fountain of grace"; law "directeth, commandeth and humbleth," gospel to "melt the heart into kindness."

**Secondary scholarship worth knowing exists:** Stephen J. Casselli, *Divine Rule Maintained: Anthony Burgess, Covenant Theology, and the Place of the Law in Reformed Scholasticism* (Reformation Heritage, 2015) — the dedicated modern monograph; also Casselli, "God the Lawgiver According to the Westminster Divine Burgess," *Unio Cum Christo* 4.1 (2018); David R. Como, *Blown by the Spirit* (Stanford, 2004), the standard history of the Antinomian underground; David Parnham, "Motions of Law and Grace," *WTJ* 70.1 (2008).

**Important caution, not just a footnote:** a two-part examination of this exact text ran on R. Scott Clark's Heidelblog in April 2025 ("To Obey Or Not To Obey..."). Clark is this project's single most substantial tracked *defender* of Kline's republication/2K theology (see `research/contemporary-interlocutors-map.md`) — his site has an institutional stake in how a Westminster-divine's law/gospel theology gets read, and the piece may (consciously or not) frame Burgess in ways favorable to a law-gospel-distinction-heavy, Escondido-adjacent reading. It's real, substantive secondary material worth having — but read Burgess's own text first and form an independent judgment before letting that framing set your expectations.

## What the source actually argues

Taking Romans 2:14-15 as the central text, Burgess begins by distinguishing two ways in which scripture speaks about the law being written on man's heart (Jeremiah 31:31-34 and Romans 2:14-15). He interprets the former to be speaking about the inclination in the will or affections towards the good—clearly a work of the Spirit, whereas the latter is describing that the law in man's heart means he possesses knowledge and judgement to identify good and evil, and it is this latter he intends to demonstrate, define, and apply. People evidence this law written in their hearts externally by forming and following laws based upon it, and internally when their conscience convicts or excuses them. He defines this law as having only two essential components: it directs or serves as a rule, and possesses obligative force from God. He then gives four particulars of the Law of Nature: it consists in common notions, like first principles—unproven, it was perfectly held in prelapsarian Adam, but fragmented in us; it is innate in man; and he attempts to set its extent or bounds in four ways (i.e. things agreed upon between man and beasts, *jus Gentium*, by reason, by positive declarations of the will of God). On the bounds and extend of natural law, none are compelling and satisfactory, for of these fail for various reasons to be a consistent measure, and to identify the postive declarations of God's will with this natural law would be to confuse conclusions for it's premises. Against the antinomians, he argues that natural law has it's source of binding authority from God, and that it is perpetually binding on all men. Lastly he gives three uses: for instruction of Antinomians specifically, for reproof to all who live contrary to the dictates of natural law, and lastly shows how an understanding of natural law explains why belief in Christ is difficult since it is not something observed from nature.


## Key quotes

> In the next place, there are two consequents of the Law which are ad bene esse, ‘that the Law may be better obeyed’; and this indeed turns the Law into a covenant, which is another notion upon it, as afterwards is to be shown. Now as for the sanction of the Law by way of a promise, that is a mere free thing; God, by reason of that dominion which he had over man, might have commanded his obedience, and yet never have made a promise of eternal life unto him. —[Pro Rege transcription](https://pro-rege.com/2025/12/12/how-is-this-law-said-to-be-written-in-their-hearts-burges/)

> The differences of its being in Adam and in us. This is necessary to observe; for it was perfectly implanted in Adam’s heart, but we have only some fragments, and a mere shadow of it left in us. —ibid

> The whole Law of Nature, as it was perfectly teaching us the will of God, was then communicated to him: and howsoever God, for good reasons hereafter to be mentioned, did give, besides that Law of Nature, a positive law to try his obedience; yet the other cannot be denied to have been in him, seeing he was made after God’s image, in righteousness, and holiness, and otherwise Adam would have been destitute of the light of reason, and without a conscience.

> Some bind [The Law of Nature] by reason in every man: but this is very uncertain, and one man’s reason is contrary to another’s, and one man’s conscience is larger than another’s; even as it is with measures in diverse countries, though they have the same name, as a bushel, etc. yet they are different in quantity, one is larger than another. —ibid

> Lastly, Others bound it by the will of God, declared and manifested to Noah in seven precepts, and afterwards to Moses in the Ten Commandments: but these extend the Law of Nature not only to first principles, but conclusions also deduced from thence. —ibid

> The obligation of it, when the Law of Nature does bind: and that is from God the author of it, God only is under no law. Every believer, though justified by Christ, is under the Moral Law of Moses, as also the law of Nature: but now this Law of Nature does not so properly bind, as it’s man’s reason or conscience, as that it is the vicegerent of God, or a command from him: and thus Cain by the Law of Nature found a tie upon him not to sin, and guilt because he did sin in murdering his brother, although there was not Moral Law as yet given. It is true, indeed, our Divines do well reprove the papists, for calling all that time from Adam to Moses, as state, or Law of Nature: and this the papists do, that therefore to offer sacrifice unto God may be proved from the Law of Nature; whereas those sacrifices being done in faith, had the word of God, otherwise we were bound still to offer lambs and kids to God, which they deny.

> Does not Nature condemn lying, cozening in your trades, lusts, and uncleanness?

## Bearing on the project's hypothesis

**The project's working hypothesis, in short** (full version in `research/natural-law-synthesis-reading-map.md`, "How the four strands might connect"): Kline/VanDrunen's "common kingdom" needs natural law to do two distinct things, and they have to be kept separate rather than run together as one claim about "neutrality" — which is exactly the conflation this Burgess lecture helped catch. **(a1) Universal operation**: natural law must be really present and really binding in every conscience, regenerate and unregenerate alike. This the Reformed tradition affirms without hesitation, and so does this project — nothing here is arguing natural law fails to reach the unregenerate. **(a2) Autonomous/neutral ground of authority**: natural law must additionally be a free-standing moral order whose validity doesn't depend on, or point back to, the true God specifically, such that it can function as genuinely neutral common ground between true religion, false religion, and no religion at all. This the tradition denies, and it's the actual load-bearing claim Kline's "common kingdom" needs and doesn't have. This Burgess lecture is Strand 1 material specifically, and it bears on both — his account of the law written on the heart (direction and obligation) is a direct test of whether he grounds natural law's authority as autonomous or as derivative of God's own, and his treatment of reason and *jus gentium* as inadequate bounds bears on how uniformly reliable (a1) actually is in practice, which is a related but separate question from (a2).

This piece supports that natural law is accessible only in a religious manner in man: natural law in the heart of man is given as a "vicegenrent of God" and has its authority from him directly. He hedges significantly the clearness of natural law to both reason and by looking at the *jus Gentium*, saying both are insufficient to ground the extend and bounds of the Law of Nature.

Burgess doesn't seem to favor one measure for bounding the extent of natural law over others, but his reasoning for the declared will of God being the wrong measure is that it "extend[s] the Law of Nature not only to first principles, but conclusions also deduced from thence." Burgess explains the limitation of reason as a bound is that some men have more of reason or stronger conscience than others, so the extent of natural law is hard to define by that standard. This is not so much about clearness as it is about the diversity of access: "Some bind it by reason in every man: but this is very uncertain, and one man’s reason is contrary to another’s, and one man’s conscience is larger than another’s; even as it is with measures in diverse countries, though they have the same name, as a bushel, etc. yet they are different in quantity, one is larger than another."

Also supportive of the hypothesis is Burgess' point regarding the obligative force of natural law in the murder of Abel by Cain prior to any declared will of God (it seems that he means Moral Law in this specific passage to refer to the positive declarations of the will of God: e.g. 7 precepts of Noah, Decalogue). Clearly, and importantly, Burgess sees the natural law definitively operative prior to the giving of the law at Sinai in a sufficient manner to oblige Cain not to sin, and to convict him of the guilt of murder.

## Open questions raised

- Would be interested in more detail regarding common notions, the medium etc. 
- Want to know more about the distinction between speculative principles of natural law and practical. For instance, perhaps he means the same common notion can imply speculative import and practical: e.g. speculative: that there is a God; practical: That God should be worshipped. Seems the practical is somewhat practical in itself and vice versa for the practical. **(This turned out to connect directly to Barker's own chapter — see his reading note and the standing strand-level question now in `research/natural-law-synthesis-reading-map.md`, Strand 1: does natural theology and natural law actually stay separable, or does each source keep fusing them the way Burgess's "that there is a God" common notion and Barker's own law-shaped definition of natural theology both do?)**
- Burgess mentions almost in passing that only fragments of the Law of Nature as it was given to Adam remain in us. How extensive is the reduction/corruption in us post-fall? What remains? What is the nature of the corruption? In what way is it reduced, e.g. categorically, quantitatively?
- "Lastly, Others bound it by the will of God, declared and manifested to Noah in seven precepts, and afterwards to Moses in the Ten Commandments: but these extend the Law of Nature not only to first principles, but conclusions also deduced from thence." I'm not entirely sure what Burgess means by conclusions vs first principles here. I think he means these are too defined to rest at the level of common notions, we would agree they are more like summations of the moral law.
- In the quote above, regarding Cain having from nature a tie not to sin and guilt for having murdered, is Moral Law being used to refer to the Decalogue? Or just that there was not yet a divine command positively given yet not to murder (as there was given to Noah)? Seems that if the former, God had at least warned him that the fruit of his anger (implied to be murder) ought to be avoided. Maybe this fits the bill as a divine precept prohibiting murder?
- Burgess distinguishes positive moral law from natural moral law and states that the difference is that the one needs and can accept of no proof: such as there is no reason given for these natural moral laws, and even lists murder as an example. But we are told we are to punish murderers because man is made in the image of God. We are also told to honor our father and our mother "that our days may be long upon the land." I was at first wondering whether these were explanations, but in hindsight, I think the answer is that these fit more as things *ad bene esse* rather than of the essence of the law itself (my own extension of his ad bene esse distinction, not stated by Burgess directly). In the case of the punishment of the murderer, the reason given pertains more to the reason for the severe consequence rather than a proof that it is wrong *per se*, and in the case of the fifth commandment, this is a promise of blessing to encourage obedience to something already required by the moral law and unproven in itself.
