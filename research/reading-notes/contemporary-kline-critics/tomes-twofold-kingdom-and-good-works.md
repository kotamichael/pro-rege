## "The Twofold Kingdom of Christ" (postnuance.substack.com/p/the-twofold-kingdom-of-christ)

Directly and extensively critiques VanDrunen's two-kingdoms framework (does not cite Kline by name). Core argument: classical Reformed theology holds Christ's kingship is **singular but modally differentiated** — essential kingdom (the eternal Son ruling all creatures by virtue of deity) and mediatorial kingdom (the incarnate Son ruling as appointed God-man to gather and defend the church) are "not two sovereignties, two moral universes, or two territories. They are two aspects of the reign of the same divine person."

**Key charge against VanDrunen:** organizing these into "two parallel kingdoms, a common civil kingdom governed by the Son as Creator and a redemptive ecclesiastical kingdom governed by Christ as mediator... risks identifying the essential kingdom with nonchurch life and the mediatorial kingdom with the institutional church" — a **spatial** misreading of what classical Reformed sources actually held to be a **modal** distinction (title, purpose, mode — not geography). Cites **David Dickson** directly against VanDrunen's spatial reading: the mediator's authority extends "over all things... in, and for the church," not confined to institutional boundaries. (Dickson is already in this project's own bibliography via *Therapeutica Sacra*, currently featured on pro-rege.com — a genuinely useful cross-reference.)

**Historiographical charge, matching this project's own confessional discipline exactly:** Tomes argues VanDrunen misreads history by treating Reformed magistratical care of religion (i.e., establishmentarianism) as "inconsistencies between theory and practice," with later American disestablishment presented as the doctrine's "more coherent fulfillment" — when in fact **the 1646 original joined institutional differentiation to confessional magistracy, and the 1788 American revision was a substantive revision, not a clarification.** This is precisely the 1646-vs-American-revision distinction this project's own house rules already insist on (WCF 23 is the flagged locus).

**On natural law specifically:** Tomes holds natural law and Scripture "reveal one moral order through distinct modes" — natural law discloses creational norms accessible to reason; Scripture clarifies and corrects fallen understanding of the same order. This is close in spirit to (though independently argued from) this project's own working hypothesis that natural law was never meant to be religiously neutral or separable from Christ's own government.

**Direct quotes:**
> "There is one sovereign Son and one created order."

> "No statute forgives sin, no election raises the dead, and no nation becomes the heavenly city." Yet Augustine's eschatological restraint "does not produce religious neutrality. The cities are formed by loves, and loves receive social expression through laws, customs, worship, education."

> "Every authority is real because God institutes it. Every authority is limited because no creaturely office possesses universal competence."

## "Good Works and Final Salvation in Reformed Orthodoxy" (postnuance.substack.com/p/good-works-and-final-salvation-in)

A dense primary-source harvest on whether good works play any causal/meritorious role in a believer's final salvation — a different specific question from Smyth's (Smyth: could unfallen Adam merit? this piece: do a regenerate believer's works merit glorification?) but the same underlying commitment (no strict human merit before God) and a huge supply of additional primary citations beyond what Smyth quoted. Companion piece exists — **"Good Works in Reformed Orthodoxy: Primary Source Documentation"** (posted the same day) is likely the raw citation compilation this essay draws on; not yet fetched, worth reading next.

**Core distinction:** *right* (title to life, acquired by Christ alone, received by faith alone) vs. *possession* (the ordained path to enjoying that life, in which works are a necessary — not meritorious — means). Voetius: believers are not bound to obedience "under the form of a means and condition of acquiring" eternal life, but are bound "under the form of gratitude, means, way, and condition of approaching and actually taking possession."

**Merit denied outright**, with three stated reasons (compiled from Polanus and others): no proportion between finite works and eternal life; works are not properly ours but God's gracious gifts; our works are impure and cannot stand before God's strict judgment. Polanus: "No works of ours can, by their own nature, merit anything from God or bind him to give a reward." Ames: "The reward of good works is not of merit, but of grace and promise."

**Extensive citation list** (all worth cross-referencing against Smyth's Turretin/Bavinck/Calvin citations for a fuller primary-source base on the same underlying question): Turretin, Voetius, Bullinger, Davenant, Maresius, Polanus, Ames, Rollock, Witsius, Owen, De Moor, Maccovius, Dickson, Edwards, plus WCF, Heidelberg Catechism, and Belgic Confession directly.

**Explicitly does not discuss:** Kline, the Adamic covenant of works directly, or condign/congruent merit terminology by name — this essay is "historically descriptive, not prescriptive or polemical toward later debates," per its own scope. Don't over-read it as a direct answer to Kline; it's adjacent ammunition, not a targeted rebuttal.

## "Grace Perfects Nature" (postnuance.substack.com/p/grace-perfects-nature) — briefer note

Constructive Strand-1-adjacent piece: "grace does not destroy nature but perfects it" (citing Alsted directly on this point), with Polanus distinguishing natural moral good "proceeding from the natural light remaining in fallen humanity" from grace-wrought moral good — i.e., some real natural knowledge/capacity survives the fall, though corrupted. Discusses Aquinas extensively as the inherited framework Reformed theology revises rather than discards. Does not engage Van Til or 2K by name, despite covering adjacent nature/grace territory.
