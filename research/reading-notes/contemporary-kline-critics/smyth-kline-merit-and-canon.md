## The highest-value finding: "Merit and Meredith" (thread 8, Jun 2023)

Direct quote from Kline's "Covenant Theology Under Attack" (unedited version, meredithkline.com — the New Horizons/OPC magazine version was "heavily edited in ways Kline disapproved," per Smyth):

> "Jesus, the second Adam, accomplishes the probationary assignment of overcoming the devil... By this achievement of active obedience he merits for us a position beyond probation, secure forever in God's love and the prospect of God's eternal home." — Meredith G. Kline

Smyth's analysis: Kline reasons *backward* from Christ's merit (as the second Adam) to insist the first Adam must likewise have been capable of **strict, properly meritorious** works in the garden — otherwise the two-Adam typology breaks. Smyth calls this "category confusion," and cites Kline directly conceding the stakes he's placed on it: in *Kingdom Prologue*, pp. 107-108, Kline states that denying "simple justice" (real human merit) **"stands condemned as subversive of that gospel."**

Smyth's counter, with citations: historic Reformed theology denied that even unfallen Adam could do *strictly and properly* meritorious works before God — not chiefly because of sin, but because of the Creator/creature distinction itself (no creature can put God in its debt; Adam already owed God perfect obedience regardless of any covenant; the disproportion between finite works and the promised reward is infinite). Cited: **Turretin, *Institutes of Elenctic Theology* 17.5.6**; **Bavinck, *Reformed Dogmatics* 2.570**; **WCF 7.1** ("which Kline wanted revised," per Smyth — a direct, serious claim worth verifying independently) and **WCF 16.5** ("our best works... cannot merit pardon of sin... by reason of the disproportion... and the infinite distance between us and God"); Calvin's comment on Luke 17:7-10 (any divine reward for our works is gracious favor, not payment for inherent worth).

Smyth's closing point: by Kline's own stated standard ("subversive of the gospel" to deny strict merit), Turretin, Bavinck, Calvin, and the Westminster divines themselves would stand condemned — which Smyth takes as evidence something has gone wrong in Kline's framing, not in the tradition's.

## "Various Reformed authors rejecting the idea of man meriting before God" (thread 9, Dec 2024)

A quick-reference list (thin on argument, useful as a citation index): **Ursinus, Turretin, Bavinck, Vos, Calvin**, and **WCF 16.5** are all cited as denying strict human merit before God, "not only in our sin but also in the Creator/creature distinction" — i.e., the same point extends even to a hypothetical unfallen state, not merely post-fall inability.

## "Historical Revisionism" (thread 10, Dec 2024) — the methodological point

Smyth's argument here is really about historiographical method, and is worth internalizing for this project's own historical-researcher discipline, not just as ammunition against Kline: finding *terminological* overlap between Kline and older Reformed sources on "republication" or "covenant of works" proves little unless the *underlying* doctrines of grace, merit, law, and the Creator/creature relation are actually shared. His analogy: superficial resemblance (both cars and airplanes have engines and wheels) doesn't survive actually taking the systems apart. Smyth explicitly credits **Lee Irons** as an honest exception: Irons openly acknowledges he is *redefining* "merit" rather than continuing the tradition's usage, citing Irons's own paper:

> **Lee Irons, "Redefining Merit: An Examination of Medieval Presuppositions in Covenant Theology"** — found via docslib.org (a document-mirror site); worth locating a cleaner copy. Title alone concedes the redefinition project Smyth is describing — a genuinely valuable, fair source to read and cite directly, since it's the defender's own words admitting the move rather than this project inferring it.

## Bearing on the project's argument

This is arguably the single richest contemporary source found in the whole author-verification audit — not because it's the most careful or systematic (it's X-thread material, occasionally thin on context), but because it supplies **direct, page-cited quotations from Kline's own primary texts** on the question of merit, which sits underneath — and is more foundational than — the covenant-of-works-at-Sinai and natural-law/2K questions this project has been tracking. **WCF 7.1's "voluntary condescension" is the very first sentence of the doctrine this whole project defends**; if Kline explicitly wanted that paragraph revised and argued that denying strict human merit is "subversive of the gospel," that is a direct, load-bearing point of confrontation this project should engage early and carefully — likely worth its own attention alongside (or even prior to) the four natural-law strands, since merit/grace's definition is logically prior to any question about Sinai's administration.
