## Structure of the chapter

Seven sections: **I.** Understanding R2K (Kline's Intrusion Ethics, then the "modern" R2K restatement) — **II.** R2K Deviates from Traditional Reformed Theology (the *duplex regnum Christi* material) — **III.** R2K Stands (and Falls) on an Erroneous Division of the Noahic Covenant (the seven arguments) — **IV.** R2K Wrongly Restricts the Use of Natural Law by the Civil Government — **V.** R2K's Novel and Contra-Confessional Rejection of Sabbath Laws — **VI.** R2K as an Accommodation to Pluralism — **VII.** Conclusion. The user's question concerns III and IV specifically, so those get full treatment below; I–II and V–VII are summarized more briefly since they were already covered adequately in the prior pass.

## I. Kline's Intrusion Ethics and the modern R2K restatement

Garris's opening move is to separate what Kline himself held from what "modern R2K" (chiefly VanDrunen) has built on it — careful, not flattening. Kline's own three load-bearing premises, per Garris: (1) the Noahic covenant grounds civil ethics in the "common grace" age; (2) Israel's theocratic ethics (Mosaic law, the imprecatory Psalms) were an "Intrusion" of *consummation* ethics, not a rule for the church or civil government today — Kline's own words: "Our thesis is that this Old Testament ethical pattern is an aspect of the Intrusion," and, more sharply, "the Old Testament is not the canon of the Christian church"; (3) the Decalogue's First Table (worship of God) should have no bearing on civil government today. Garris's blunt verdict on all three: "we also contend that Kline has not established any of these premises from Scripture."

Garris also documents, via a footnote already flagged in this project's prior pass, that Kline himself held the American revision to WCF 23.3 to have introduced "inner tension" against the *original* 1646 sense of WCF 19.4 — i.e., Kline agreed that unrevised 19.4 placed the first four commandments under civil jurisdiction, and regarded the 1788 American text as a "patchwork job" that changed the confession's meaning. This is a genuinely useful, directly quoted confirmation for this project's own 1646-vs-American-revision discipline (CLAUDE.md's provisional house rules already flag WCF 23 as "the main case" of this exact issue) — Kline himself is a witness for the same textual point this project makes, even though he draws the opposite constructive conclusion from it.

The "modern R2K" restatement (via VanDrunen) recasts Kline's "common grace ethics"/"consummation ethics" split as a **"common kingdom"** (founded on the Noahic covenant, encompassing civil government, family, education, art — governed by natural law) vs. a **"redemptive kingdom"** (founded on the Abrahamic covenant, encompassing the church — governed by Scripture). Garris quotes John Frame's 1990 summary approvingly for its clarity: "the Noahic covenant establishes a religiously neutral system of government, i.e., one in which no religion is to have an advantage over any other." Garris's framing of what's actually at stake: this is not "the separation of church and state" but "a separation of religion and state" — the state becomes religiously neutral by design, not merely institutionally distinct from the church.

## II. The *duplex regnum Christi* material (brief)

Garris leans heavily on Jonathon Beeke's *Duplex Regnum Christi: Christ's Twofold Kingdom in Reformed Theology* (Brill, 2020) to argue R2K's "two kingdoms" language is actually closer to *Luther's* early terminology (a plurality of kingdoms) than to the Reformed orthodox's own *duplex regnum* — where Christ's essential kingdom (as God) and mediatorial kingdom (as God-man) were distinguished **by mode of covenantal administration, not by scope or boundary**, and were held to "comprise one kingdom that is universal in scope." This is directly relevant to this project's own Strand 3 material (Gillespie/Gib vs. Symington on mediatorial vs. essential grounding of Christ's civil authority) — Garris/Beeke's point that the orthodox distinction was never a *scope*-limiting one is a real data point for that fork, worth reading Beeke's monograph directly if Strand 3 goes further.

## III. "R2K Stands (and Falls) on an Erroneous Division of the Noahic Covenant" — the Seven Arguments, in full

**The stakes, per Garris:** "The division of Genesis 6–9 into two separate covenants is the sine qua non of R2K theology, and therefore, to refute this division would be to undermine R2K entirely." Kline (*Kingdom Prologue*, 230–34) and VanDrunen split Genesis 6:18 (a covenant made with Noah alone, singular "you," promising survival through the flood) from Genesis 9:8-17 (a covenant made with Noah, his offspring, and "every living creature," promising no future worldwide flood) — treating the first as *redemptive* (part of the covenant of grace) and the second as *non-redemptive*, "common grace" only, and therefore the biblical foundation for R2K's religiously-neutral "common kingdom." VanDrunen's own words, quoted directly: "The evidence strongly points to the conclusion that this [Gen. 6:18] is a different covenant from the one mentioned in Genesis 9," and, elsewhere, "I stake much of my political theology on the distinctive nature and purposes of the Noahic covenant." Garris notes this division is **not universal even among Kline/VanDrunen-sympathetic scholars** — in the same multi-author volume VanDrunen and Miles Van Pelt both draw on (*Covenant Theology*, eds. Waters/Reid/Muether, 2020), the very next chapter's author, John Scott Redd, states flatly that "the Noahic covenant is singular and complete even though it is administered at different points."

Garris's seven arguments for a single, unified Noahic covenant:

1. **The Hebrew verb for "establish."** Both Gen. 6:18 and Gen. 9 use *heqim berit* ("confirm/establish a covenant"), not *karat berit* ("cut a covenant" — the verb for *initiating* a new covenant). Citing Gentry and Wellum's lexical analysis (*Kingdom through Covenant*), *heqim* consistently means "a covenant partner fulfilling an obligation or upholding a promise in a covenant initiated previously." The same pattern appears with Abraham: God "cuts" (*karat*) the covenant in Gen. 15:18, then "confirms" (*heqim*) it with the sign of circumcision in Gen. 17. On this reading, Genesis 9 doesn't inaugurate a second covenant; it **confirms/ratifies** the one already announced in Gen. 6:18 — whether what's being confirmed there is the Gen. 3:15 protoevangelium (so Stephen Myers) or God's care for creation more generally (so Gentry/Wellum) is itself disputed, but either way both texts point back to one antecedent commitment.
2. **Both passages involve all creation, not just Noah's family.** Gen. 6:18-20 already speaks of "every living thing of all flesh," clean and unclean animals — not merely a private promise to one man. The parallel wording between 6:18-20 and 9:9-11 (both use covenant-plus-offspring-plus-"every living creature" language) undercuts the claim that one passage is narrowly personal and the other universal.
3. **Genesis 8 and 9 can't be cleanly cut.** The "be fruitful and multiply" command (Gen. 9:1, 7) is already anticipated for the animals in Gen. 8:16-17 — the creational-renewal language spans the supposed covenant boundary. Notably, even **Kline himself** wrote that "the Noahic community was a family, not a nation to which a kingdom-treaty might appropriately be directed" (*The Structure of Biblical Authority*, 77) — a concession Garris reads as working against R2K's own application of the passage.
4. **Structural parallel to the Abrahamic covenant.** Both Noah and Abraham receive a life-promise, ratified by covenant, followed by a crisis threatening that promise (flood; Ishmael/an illegitimate heir), then sealed with a sign (the rainbow; circumcision) — a unified narrative shape that assumes one covenant story, not two spliced together.
5. **Genesis 9:6's death penalty directly answers Genesis 6's stated problem.** God floods the earth because it was "filled with violence" (Gen. 6:11, 13); the death penalty for murder in 9:6 is the solution to precisely that violence. This ties Genesis 9 back into Genesis 6's own narrative logic rather than starting a fresh, unrelated covenant.
6. **A redemptive promise closes out Genesis 9.** Beyond the no-more-flood promise (9:14-15), the chapter ends with the Shem/Japheth blessing (9:26-27) — read by Witsius (quoted at length, citing *The Economy of the Covenants*) as both literal (Greek/Roman expansion into Asia) and typological (the Gentile nations, "Japheth," welcomed into the tents of "Shem," i.e., the church). This is a genealogical-redemptive payoff, not a merely "common"/secular one.
7. **Noah's sacrifice (Gen. 8:20) is inseparable from the covenant confirmed in Genesis 9.** VanDrunen concedes "the substantive similarity across the sections" and that Gen. 8:20-22 is "an important aid in interpreting the covenantal words spoken to Noah [Gen. 9:8-17]" — but tries to keep the sacrifice itself under the "first," redemptive covenant of 6:18 while denying it colors Genesis 9. Garris presses that this is exegetically unsustainable: God's promise not to curse the ground again is *explicitly given in response to smelling* the sacrifice's "pleasing aroma" (Gen. 8:20-21) — the sacrifice is the hinge the whole subsequent covenant swings on, not a separable prior episode. Worth noting for this project's own confidence-marking: **even Kline** described Noah's sacrifice as belonging to "the consummated kingdom," praising the "King of kings" (*Kingdom Prologue*, 229) — another point where Garris shows Kline's own language cutting against the R2K use of his division.

**The clincher Garris keeps returning to:** even Kline states outright that Genesis 9's purpose was to *serve redemption* — "The objective of this covenant was to subserve the divine purpose and program of redemptive covenant and thereby, indirectly, the coming of the holy kingdom" (*Kingdom Prologue*, 261), and "the common grace covenant of Genesis 8:20–9:17 was designed to provide the historical order and set the world stage for the renewed program of redemptive covenant" (262). Garris's point: this is functionally the *same conclusion* held by unified-covenant advocates like O. Palmer Robertson (Genesis 9 provides "a context of preservation in which the grace of redemption may operate") and Richard Belcher ("The primary purpose of the covenant with Noah was to provide for the continuation of creation so God's redemptive program of salvation... could be carried out") — meaning **Kline's own stated purpose for the "common grace covenant" already concedes the redemptive framing** his own division was constructed to avoid. Conclusion of this section: "Genesis 9 is not a mere 'common grace' covenant but involves sacrifices related to redemption... this is one covenant, harmonizing creational language with God's redemption. It is a preservation of creation for the purpose of redemption."

## IV. "R2K Wrongly Restricts the Use of Natural Law by the Civil Government" — the natural-law/natural-theology core

This is the section most directly on point for this project's own Strand 1/2 hypothesis, and Garris's argument here is a genuine internal-tension critique, not just a disagreement of emphasis.

**Step one — VanDrunen's own definition of natural law already includes worship of God (First Table).** Garris quotes VanDrunen directly, twice: "Natural law refers to the law of God made known in the created order, which all human beings know through their physical senses, intellect, and conscience..." and, "there are universal moral obligations binding upon all people in all places, and that all people at some level know these obligations even without biblical revelation, because they are grounded in human nature." Garris's point: since Rom. 1:18-32 makes knowledge of God (and obligation toward him) part of what "all people in all places" know, VanDrunen's own definition of natural law *already* includes the First Table, not just the Second. VanDrunen concedes as much elsewhere, in his own words: Calvin "saw natural law as the standard for civil law, and yet he...identified natural law with the Ten Commandments," and, "Reformed orthodoxy associated natural law very closely with the moral law and the Decalogue" — citing Turretin's own claim that natural law and the Decalogue "differ not in substance or principles, but in 'mode of delivery.'" VanDrunen even grants that on this basis, historic Reformed orthodoxy "could claim to have a natural law basis for entrusting the magistrate with the establishment and enforcement of laws concerning atheism, idolatry, blasphemy, and worship" (his own words, quoted in full).

**Step two — but then VanDrunen restricts natural law's civil-jurisdiction scope anyway, via the Noahic covenant.** Having granted that natural law includes the First Table, VanDrunen argues the Noahic covenant grants a "penultimate natural law right to religious freedom before... fellow human beings" (even while denying any "ultimate natural law right to religious freedom" before God himself) — meaning the civil sphere is bound only by natural law's "bare minimalist ethic" (VanDrunen's own phrase) covering "intrahuman relations": procreation, eating, capital justice for murder. VanDrunen's own justification, quoted at length: "the minimalist natural law ethic, made explicit in Genesis 9:1-7, concerns intrahuman relations that represent basic requirements for the preservation of human society... worshipping the true God in the right way is not" [necessary for society's bare survival]. Garris's rebuttal: **this is a bifurcation of natural law's *content* from natural law's *civil jurisdiction*, smuggled in through the Noahic covenant rather than argued from the nature of natural law itself.** Garris's own summary syllogism of VanDrunen's stated premises: (1) civil government must apply WCF 19.4's "general equity"; (2) "general equity," per VanDrunen himself, "is, for all intents and purposes, a reference to natural law"; (3) natural law, per VanDrunen's own citation of the Standards, is summarized in the whole Decalogue, First Table included — **conclusion (which VanDrunen does not draw): WCF 19.4 obligates civil government to the Decalogue's First Table too.** Garris: "according to VanDrunen's own concessions, the Westminster Confession teaches that civil government should enforce laws related to the First Table" — and this "was also the conclusion of VanDrunen's mentor, Meredith Kline" (per the WTJ footnote already noted above).

**Three specific rebuttals to VanDrunen's stated reasons for the First/Second Table civil split:**
1. The Noahic covenant's *silence* on First Table matters doesn't imply their *prohibition* — Genesis 9 doesn't even legislate most Second Table crimes (adultery, theft) or distinguish murder from manslaughter (Num. 35 does that later), yet VanDrunen wouldn't say those distinctions are therefore forbidden to civil government. If reason and later revelation may fill gaps for Second Table matters, the same logic should permit it for First Table matters.
2. VanDrunen's claim that Genesis 9 is "too sparse" for doctrinal/civil adjudication, so Scripture generally shouldn't guide the magistrate, proves too much — earlier Genesis chapters already establish monotheism, and VanDrunen's own political theology already draws on later special revelation (Genesis 9 itself) to *limit* magistrates; there's no principled reason later revelation couldn't also *expand* their guidance.
3. VanDrunen's appeal to the image of God (Gen. 1:26-28; 9:6) as grounding a *prohibition* on religious establishment is, in Garris's word, "inane" — image-bearing grounds the magistrate's duty to rule *for* his subjects' good, and since Christianity is good for a person's soul, image-of-God reasoning should point *toward* directing subjects to the true religion, not toward protecting idolatry.

**Garris's own constructive natural-law claim, worth flagging directly for this project's a1/a2 hypothesis:** "it makes little sense to say that we can derive laws from nature but not derive the Lawgiver from nature. Nor does it make sense to recognize natural law as law and not recognize a Lawgiver... The Second Table is built upon the First Table. God is essential to an orderly society, and government must acknowledge this." This is close kin to — arguably a sharper, more compressed version of — this project's own (a2) denial: natural law's operation is real and universal (a1), but that operation cannot be sealed off from the true God as its own ground and still function as a "neutral" or "common" standard (denying a2). Garris arrives at essentially the same denial from the direction of civil jurisdiction rather than epistemology, which is a useful independent confirmation of the same fault line.

## V–VII. Sabbath laws, pluralism-as-accommodation, conclusion (brief)

**V.** Garris argues the Westminster Standards (WLC 118, 121, 124, 129–30; WCF 23.2–3 [1788]) obligate civil "superiors" to enforce Sabbath observance, and that VanDrunen's stated reason for rejecting Sabbath laws — "it's really only Christians who can understand why we rest on the first day of the week" (quoted from a VanDrunen/Kevin DeYoung interview) — proves too much, since plenty of laws (e.g., against polygamy) don't require full non-Christian comprehension to be justly enforced.

**VI.** Garris reads R2K as sociologically, not just exegetically, motivated: "a defeatist response to the advances of theological liberalism and religious pluralism in the West," citing VanDrunen's own admission that his position "has modern features that are sympathetic to some of the broad goals of the Enlightenment." A lengthy footnote critiques the ESV's rendering of Greek *paroikia*/*parepidemos* as "exile" (vs. the KJV's "sojourner"/"pilgrim/stranger"), arguing the "exile" framing subtly justifies political passivity — a philological point worth flagging for this project's own KJV-default house rule, since it's an independent, non-polemical reason (translation accuracy, not just tradition) to prefer the KJV's rendering here.

**VII.** Conclusion restates the double charge: R2K rests on "an erroneous interpretation and application of the Noahic Covenant, as well as an erroneous understanding of natural law's relationship to civil government."

## Bearing on the project's argument

This chapter is now this project's richest single source of *direct, quoted* VanDrunen material (not secondary characterization) on exactly the two questions the natural-law strands care about most: (1) whether the Noahic covenant can be split into a redemptive and a religiously-neutral half, and (2) whether natural law's civil jurisdiction can be narrowed to the Second Table while its *content* (per VanDrunen's own admission) already includes the First. Two points connect directly to this project's own sharpened hypothesis:

- Garris's natural-law argument in section IV is, structurally, an (a1)/(a2) argument even though he never uses that language: he grants natural law's universal operation *and content* extends to the First Table (a1, and VanDrunen agrees), but shows VanDrunen smuggling in a *jurisdictional* neutrality (a2-adjacent — natural law functioning as though religiously non-binding once in civil hands) that isn't licensed by (a1) alone. This is a different angle of attack than Marino's objective/subjective "common" distinction (already tracked in `marino-covenant-theology-video-critique.md`) but converges on the same target.
- The "even Kline says Genesis 9 serves redemption" move (section III) is a genuinely strong internal-consistency argument — worth citing directly if this project ever needs a single, tightly-sourced case that Kline's own textual admissions don't fully support the uses later R2K writers make of his division.

## Open questions raised

Worth comparing directly against Burgess's own hedged language on natural law's *bounds* being hard to measure.
- Garris's seven-arguments section leans on disputed philology (*heqim* vs. *karat berit*) as argument #1 — is this reading of Gentry/Wellum's lexical claim actually settled among Hebraists, or is it one contested position among several? Worth flagging as UNVERIFIED until checked against a source besides Garris's own citation of it.
- The chapter's broader political program (civil enforcement of First Table laws, Sabbath laws, "Christian nation") goes well beyond what this project needs to establish for its own natural-law argument — worth being explicit, per this project's non-goals, that citing Garris's Noahic/natural-law exegesis doesn't commit this project to his full establishmentarian civil program.
