# Zachary Garris, "A Critique of Modern 'Reformed Two Kingdoms'"

**Full citation:** Chapter 6 of *Reformed Christian Politics* (Stephen Wolfe, Zachary Garris, Sean McGowan; Berith Press, 2026; ISBN 978-1-963516-62-3), originally published free online Feb. 27, 2026 as part of *"A Study Report on Reformed Christian Politics: Recovering the Politics of the Classical Protestant Tradition"* (reformedchristianpolitics.org). Authorship of individual chapters was only disclosed in the print edition's preface — confirmed this chapter is Garris's, not McGowan's (McGowan authored Ch. 2 and the Conclusion instead).

**Status: CONFIRMED** — direct quotations recovered from the full text.

## What the source argues

A direct, sustained critique of Meredith Kline's two-kingdoms theology and its "R2K" descendants (VanDrunen named explicitly as "Kline's former student" and "the leading proponent of R2K"), argued on three fronts:

1. **Confessional/general-equity grounds** — the chapter's central charge: R2K "effectively denies the 'general equity' of the First Table of the Ten Commandments per WCF 19.4," and Kline himself treated the American revision to WCF 23.3 as contradicting the original 1646 meaning of 19.4, calling the American Confession "a patchwork job" with "inner tension" (Kline's own words, quoted). This directly overlaps this project's own 1646-vs-American-revision citation discipline.
2. **Exegetical grounds** — a dedicated section, "Seven Arguments against the Klinean/R2K Division of the Noahic Covenant," arguing for a single, undivided Noahic covenant against Kline's "common kingdom"/"redemptive kingdom" bifurcation of it.
3. **Historical-theological grounds** — situates Kline's "intrusion ethics" (Israel's theocracy as a temporary "intrusion" of final judgment into common history) as the load-bearing move underneath R2K, and argues it hasn't been established from Scripture: "Yet we also contend that Kline has not established any of these premises from Scripture."

On republication specifically, the chapter is careful rather than sweeping — a footnote distinguishes "administrative" from "substantial" republication readings of Kline himself, citing the OPC's 2016 report, and notes "not all [R2K] proponents agree with his position." This is a more careful move than treating "Kline = strict substantial republication" as settled, and is worth matching in this project's own manifesto language.

## Key quotes

> "Reformed Two Kingdoms (R2K) is a modern view of civil government that has its roots in the theology of Meredith Kline (1922–2007)."

> Kline "and his followers have done no less than propose an alternative theological system to the Westminster Standards."

> "Our thesis is that this Old Testament ethical pattern is an aspect of the Intrusion" — "the Old Testament is not the canon of the Christian church" — "it is in this New Testament age not a legitimate function of a civil government to endorse and support religious establishments" (Kline, quoted).

> Conclusion: R2K is "a defeatist response to the advances of theological liberalism and religious pluralism," "novel," "ad hoc," and "detracts from the [Reformed] tradition... requires faulty assumptions and leads itself into incoherence."

## Bearing on the project's argument

This is the most substantive, directly-quoted contemporary critique of Kline turned up in the whole author-audit process — more direct than Myers/Belcher (who stay at the covenant-of-works-at-Sinai level) and argued on different ground than this project's own natural-law/mediatorial-kingship approach. Garris's method is primarily confessional (WCF 19.4 general equity) and exegetical (the Noahic covenant's unity), not a natural-law-content-and-neutrality argument — meaning it doesn't overlap with, or pre-empt, this project's own natural-law strand. Worth citing directly in the manifesto's "Relation to Existing Treatments" as a fourth, distinct angle (alongside Myers/Belcher's covenant-theology angle, Wolfe's eschatology-of-dominion angle, and this project's own natural-law angle) — good evidence there are several live, non-overlapping lines of critique in the field right now, not a single unified opposition to Kline.

Note the book's own general-equity position (Ch. 5, by Garris: "Theonomy vs. WCF General Equity") deliberately distances itself from strict Bahnsen theonomy — this is a moderate confessionalist position, not theonomic, worth not conflating the two when characterizing this circle.

## Open questions raised
