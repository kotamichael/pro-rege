## Episodes 1-2 — Romans 1:19-20, the object and audience of the knowledge

Marino frames the whole series around four questions he puts to Romans 1:19-20: (1) what is the exact *object* of this knowledge, (2) *who* is included in the audience, (3) what is the *nature* of the knowledge (innate vs. acquired, immediate vs. mediate, principial vs. discursive), and (4) does the answer imply an *objective* natural theology or only a *subjective* one. He insists on keeping verse 19 ("what may be known about God... because God has shown it") as central, not just verse 20 — because verse 19 establishes that "divine revelation always precedes human reason," guarding against a natural-theology account that makes reason the *source* rather than merely the *means* of this knowledge.

On content (Q1): working through Murray, Haldane, Calvin, Matthew Henry, Hodge, Boyce, Hendriksen, and others, he argues Paul's "eternal power and divine nature" is illustrative, not exhaustive — the created order also communicates God's justice (tying forward to Rom 1:32 and 2:14-15), wrath, goodness, wisdom, and (citing Bruce Baker) even his personal, rather than impersonal, character.

On audience (Q2): "all mankind gets the message" equally *sufficiently* for inexcusability, though not equally in *content* — a peasant, a mathematician, and a lifelong farmer all receive genuinely sufficient revelation but do not receive identical amounts of it. This distinction matters for what follows: it rules out any account that restricts general revelation to a bare, uniform, innate "package."

## Episode 2-3 — the nature of the knowledge, and objective vs. subjective natural theology

This is the series' most philosophically loaded material. Marino rejects both a purely innate/intuited account (associimg it loosely with "fideism") and a purely acquired/empiricist one, arguing (citing Turretin's own explicit denial of innate ideas, decades before Locke) that Reformed orthodoxy already held general revelation to be **both immediate and mediate** — communicated directly to the soul *and* through the discursive, reasoned contemplation of the created order — and that pitting these against each other is a false dilemma. His target throughout is the presuppositionalist claim (he cites Frame: "human beings are obligated to presuppose God in all their thinking") that any product of discursive reasoning not already licensed by a prior scriptural premise cannot count as true natural theology — a move he says empties "natural theology" of any real, objective content and reduces it to a description of fallen or redeemed *performance* with revelation, not a status of the knowledge itself.

Episode 3 states the payoff directly: natural theology must be **objective** — an object of the mind, true independent of and antecedent to any finite mind's grasp of it, the same in a regenerate and an unregenerate mind alike (he draws this out with a "law of identity" analogy: if the content differed between minds it wouldn't be knowledge at all, just as Romans 3:4, "let God be true though every man a liar," treats God's truth as invariant under human reception). He works through Barth and Van Til directly here as the two moderns who, in his reading, push hardest against this. Barth is treated as the more extreme case (natural theology dissolved entirely into "knowing that we do not know him"). **Van Til is treated more carefully and more charitably** — Marino traces a real tension in Van Til's own words between denying "actual knowledge of God's nature" survives the Fall and elsewhere affirming (against a critic, Masselink) that "all men, even after the fall, know deep down in their hearts that they are creatures of God" — and closes by quoting a passage where, in Marino's own words, "Van Till comes very close to affirming all that we would ask of him to affirm." This is a substantive point in Van Til's favor stated in Marino's own words, not just a claim this project is inferring — worth citing directly if Strand 4 needs an example of a contemporary Reformed-classicalist treating Van Til charitably rather than as a simple foil.

## Episode 4 — deliberation and inexcusability

Shorter episode, closing out the Romans 1:19-20 material and moving to Psalm 19:1-6. Key move: inexcusability requires that what is suppressed and exchanged (Rom 1:18-32) be *real, reasoned-about* content, not merely "the occasion" for guilt "like light hitting a table." Citing Owen Anderson (*The Clarity of God's Existence*) directly: if unbelief is to be culpable ignorance, God's existence must be clearly, readily knowable — an argument this project should note is functionally the same move Anderson makes in the material already tracked at Strand 1. Marino also works Psalm 19 here, arguing (via Calvin, Matthew Henry) that "the heavens declare the glory of God" is itself already a discursive, reasoned inference from created effect to divine cause, not bare poetic ornament or unreflective intuition.

## Episode 5 — Romans 2:14-15, natural law written on the heart

**The single most load-bearing episode for this project's purposes, and it delivers.** Marino first clears away readings (Cranfield, Barth, and — more surprisingly — James D.G. Dunn) that take "written on their hearts" as an echo of Jeremiah 31:33's new-covenant law-writing rather than a natural-law claim about all Gentiles as such. His counter (via Douglas Moo, F.F. Bruce, and the contrast with 2:27-29's "true Jew") is that the "Gentiles" in view in 2:14-15 are *all* Gentiles, universally, in exact parallel with "all mankind" in 1:18-32 — so the passage cannot be restricted to converted Gentiles without breaking that parallel.

The key positive move, and the one this project should weigh most heavily: Marino explicitly defines **natural law as a species of natural theology**, not a separate or parallel category — "natural law is a function, a species of natural theology," diagrammed as a smaller circle (divine *justice*, specifically) nested inside the wider circle of natural theology's objective theological "natures" (divine attributes generally) established in Romans 1:19-20. "Natural law reasoning is simply our reasoning about that which is communicated in nature" — the same formula, genus and species, he uses for natural theology as a whole.

He also does careful, technical work distinguishing **the work of the law written on the heart** from **conscience** — following Murray and Shedd, he argues Paul deliberately avoids saying "the law is written on their hearts," saying instead "the *work* of the law," precisely to keep the objective moral content (the "work of the law") distinct from conscience's subjective, variable *testimony to* that content (citing Romans 14 and 1 Cor 8-9's "weaker and stronger consciences" as proof that conscience can diverge in accuracy from the fixed moral standard it bears witness to — conscience cannot itself *be* the law, since then there could be no such thing as a wrongly calibrated conscience). Citing Wilhelmus à Brakel directly (garbled in the transcript as "broccoli"/"brockle" — see Confidence below): natural law's obligation is grounded not in reason's own verdict about good and evil ("for then it would not be the law of God but a rational statement") but in God's own will, communicated *through* reason as the means, not the source — "reason discovers, it doesn't determine." This is, verbatim in substance, the same formula already tracked from Marino's written "Theonomy & Natural Law" piece in the interlocutors map.

## Episode 6 — other supporting texts

Wider-ranging episode connecting the natural-theology thesis to narrative texts (Moses and the signs in Exodus 4, Deuteronomy 18's test of a prophet, Elijah at Carmel, Doubting Thomas, Paul at Athens in Acts 17) and other General-revelation loci (Psalm 36:9, John 1:9, Acts 14:16-17). The recurring point: Scripture itself repeatedly directs people to reason discursively about "extra-biblical objects with natures" as legitimate grounds for belief in special-revelation claims — a point aimed at presuppositionalist apologetic method more than at the two-kingdoms question, and the episode where the natural-theology series is furthest from this project's own R2K-facing concerns. Notably favorable treatment of Calvin's Mars Hill commentary (Paul's citation of pagan poets as "natural arguments," not a concession to autonomous reason) and of the "all truth is God's truth" principle via Calvin on Titus 1:12.

## Episode 7 — problem texts

Works through classic anti-natural-theology proof texts: Genesis 1:1 ("in the beginning God" as presuppositional, not argued), Psalm 14:1 ("the fool says... no God" as practical, not theoretical, atheism), 1 Corinthians 1-2 (distinguishing General/convicting knowledge of God from Special/saving knowledge as two circles, one nested in the other, rather than two univocally identical or flatly contradictory uses of "knowledge of God" — explicitly invoked to avoid "pitting Paul against Paul"), and Colossians 2:8 (arguing Paul's threefold *kata*-clause structure — "according to human tradition," "according to elemental spirits," "not according to Christ" — already distinguishes a bad, autonomous philosophy from a true, Christ-grounded one, rather than condemning philosophy as such). Closes with two Calvin quotations on receiving truth from non-Christian sources regardless of the speaker's own character.

## Episode 8 — interacting with N.T. Wright

Short, informal episode (explicitly framed by Marino as an audio test). Engages Wright's Gifford Lectures material (published as *History and Eschatology*) on natural theology, distinguishing Wright's own project — natural theology reconceived around biblically-shaped *history* and eschatology, with nature functioning as "signposts" to Christ's inaugurated kingdom — from the older "metaphysical" natural theology Marino is defending. Marino is measured: he says he has "no problem" including Wright's approach as *a* form of natural theology, objecting only if it is offered as a *replacement* for the metaphysical form rather than an addition to it. **No engagement with R2K, Kline, VanDrunen, or Van Til's political theology in this episode at all** — it is a methodological aside about apologetics/natural theology as a discipline, prompted by Wright's book, not a continuation of the covenant-theology critique.

## Bearing on the project's argument

**(1) This directly and substantially advances the Strand 1 standing question** (`research/natural-law-synthesis-reading-map.md`, added 2026-07-30, on whether natural theology and natural law stay separable). Marino's answer is neither "they fuse into one undifferentiated thing" (which is how Barker and Burgess's own language reads on its own) nor "they are two independent categories that happen to overlap." He offers a third, more precise structure: **natural law is a proper species of natural theology — genus and species, not synonyms and not two unrelated things.** All natural law is natural theology (a subset of the objective "natures" communicated about God, specifically his justice); not all natural theology is natural law (the wider circle also includes, e.g., God's eternal power, his being the "true light," his personal character). This is a genuinely useful sharpening tool to test against Barker and Burgess: Barker's move to fold "duty and service to Him" directly into his definition of natural theology, and Burgess's inclusion of "that God should be worshipped" among natural law's own foundational common notions, both look, on Marino's model, like predictable consequences of the genus/species relationship rather than evidence that the categories are simply indistinguishable — the practical/moral "species" keeps surfacing inside general statements about the "genus" because the two were never meant to be pulled fully apart in the first place, only distinguished in *kind* within a single field. Worth testing this genus/species framing directly against Augustine, Aquinas, and Turretin as the strand's reading continues — it may hold up better than a flat "fusion" reading, or it may itself be too tidy; flag either way as the reading proceeds.

**(2) This also independently extends the project's own a1/a2 hypothesis** beyond natural law specifically to natural theology as a whole. Marino's "objective vs. subjective natural theology" question (episode 3) is doing exactly the same work as this project's (a1) universal-operation vs. (a2) autonomous-neutral-authority split, but applied one level up — not just "is natural law universally binding and never neutral" but "is natural theology as such universally real and never neutral." His repeated formula ("reason discovers, it doesn't determine"; natural theology's content is "true independent of and antecedent to" any finite mind) is the theonomous-not-autonomous move stated at the more general level. This is a second, independent contemporary confirmation of the hypothesis (alongside his objective/subjective "common" distinction already tracked in the covenant-theology file) — worth citing both together if a published article wants two convergent examples of Marino making the same underlying move at two different levels of the argument.

**(3) None of the 8 episodes engage R2K/Kline/VanDrunen/Escondido by name.** This is different subject matter from the "Covenant Theology" playlist, as anticipated — it is Strand 1 material end to end (with episode 8 a brief methodological aside on Wright), not a continuation of the political-theology critique. The nearest thing to political-theological content is incidental: episode 2's aside that natural theology "grounds not only your doctrine of God but your doctrine of Christ... it grounds your systematic theology" (via a Machen quotation) gestures at the same "everything downstream depends on this" claim that animates his R2K critique elsewhere, but it is not applied to two-kingdoms doctrine here.

## Open questions raised

- Does Marino's genus/species (natural law as species of natural theology) framing actually resolve the Strand 1 fusion question, or does it just relocate the difficulty — i.e., can Augustine, Aquinas, or Turretin be shown to hold the *same* genus/species structure, or might one of them actually keep the categories more cleanly separate than Marino's model predicts? This needs testing against those primary sources directly, not assumed from Marino's essay-form argument alone.
- Marino's treatment of Van Til in episode 3 is more charitable than a first pass through the covenant-theology material might suggest — worth reconciling directly with the fuller Strand 4 material on Marino's "Common Grace Conflated" (which reads Van Til's role in the 1920s common-grace debate more critically) to see whether these are two different registers (methodological charity here, historical-diagnostic critique there) or an actual tension in how Marino treats Van Til across his corpus.
- Marino's Colossians 2:8 exegesis (three *kata*-clauses distinguishing good and bad philosophy) is a clean, quotable structure — worth checking whether any of this project's own Strand 1/4 sources (Turretin, Muller) make the same move independently.
