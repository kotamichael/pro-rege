# The covenant of works and the question of merit

Not part of the four-strand natural-law reading project (`research/natural-law-synthesis-reading-map.md`) — that project is about natural law's political/civil use, and this is prior to it: whether Adam's obedience in Eden could ever have been *strictly meritorious*, or whether reward was possible only by God's own "voluntary condescension" (WCF 7.1). Created 2026-07-29, after the Series-I-vs-Strand-5 question raised earlier was left open — this is that "third way" actually executed: **the positive doctrine belongs to Series I; contemporary departures from it (Kline) are Series IV's job**, engaged there as a clarifying pressure-test, not as this material's reason for existing.

**This is constructive material first.** The goal is stating clearly what Reformed orthodoxy actually held about Adam's covenant — condescension, not merit — from its own primary sources (Turretin, Bavinck, Calvin, Ursinus, Vos, WCF 7.1 and 16.5). Kline's contrary claim belongs here too, but as the occasion that forces the constructive statement to be precise, not as the point of gathering the material.

## Confirmed so far

- **WCF 7.1** itself: reasonable creatures "could never have any fruition of [God] as their blessedness and reward, but by some voluntary condescension on God's part." No creature, fallen or unfallen, can put God in its debt — the Creator/creature distinction itself rules out strict merit, prior to any question of sin.
- **WCF 16.5**: "our best works... cannot merit pardon of sin, or eternal life... by reason of the disproportion that is between them and the glory to come, and the infinite distance that is between us and God."
- **Meredith Kline** holds the opposite: Adam had to be capable of *strictly and properly meritorious* obedience, or Christ as the second Adam couldn't merit anything for us. States the stakes explicitly in *Kingdom Prologue*, 107-108 — denying real human merit "stands condemned as subversive of that gospel."
- **Kline's method, per Brandon Adams's analysis** (contrast2.wordpress.com, "Kline's Covenant Creation & WCF 7.1," 2015) — CONFIRMED, direct quotes on file: Kline rejects the Confession's reading of Luke 17:10 (the "unprofitable servants" text WCF 7.1 leans on), and redefines justice/merit itself: rather than the Confession's voluntarist position (God graciously chooses to bind himself to reward by covenant, not by natural desert), Kline's move is "God's justice must be defined... in terms of what he stipulates in his covenants" — collapsing the Confession's distinction between natural law (no inherent reward-structure) and covenant (which adds "do this and live"). Adams also flags cascading tension with **WCF 19.2** (the law given to Israel "as a rule of righteousness," not as a covenant of works) and **19.5-7** (Christians bound to the law while freed from it as a covenant of works) — hard to sustain if the law inherently carries a works-principle, as Kline holds.
- **Kline redefines grace itself**, independently confirmed by a second source beyond Andrew Smyth (Sinenkosi, mildyoke.wordpress.com, "The Graciousness of the Covenant of Works," 2020) — direct Kline quote: "grace is not merely the bestowal of unmerited blessings, but God's blessing of man in spite of his demerits, in spite of his forfeiture of divine blessings." On Kline's own definition, grace only operates *after* there's something to forgive — excluding the prelapsarian Adamic relationship from being gracious at all. This second source marshals Calvin, Witsius, Vos, and Bavinck against Kline on this point without claiming Kline sought the Confession's actual text revised — a point worth being precise about (see below).
- **Andrew Smyth's parallel find** (already tracked, `research/reading-notes/contemporary-kline-critics/smyth-kline-merit-and-canon.md`): same substantive tension, cites Turretin *IET* 17.5.6, Bavinck *RD* 2.570, Calvin on Luke 17:7-10.

**One claim does NOT hold up as stated:** Smyth's parenthetical that "Kline wanted WCF 7.1 revised" isn't confirmed by either Adams's or Sinenkosi's treatment — both show Kline's *position* directly contradicts 7.1's stated reasoning, but neither documents him calling for the *text* to be rewritten. That's a different, weaker, but still real claim: Kline's own confirmed complaint about the Confession needing revision is about **chapter 19.4/23.3** (general equity/civil magistrate — "a patchwork job... in a condition of inner tension," already sourced via Garris's chapter and Smyth's separate "Patchwork Confession" post), not chapter 7. Don't conflate the two.

That constructive statement is Series I's job and doesn't depend on Kline at all.
