# Pro-Rege: Manifesto

## Purpose

This is a constructive project before it is a critical one. Its aim is to reconstruct and state clearly the classical Reformed orthodox position on natural and moral law, philosophy, epistemology, and apologetics — and how each properly relates to special revelation — so that Christians can see, with real clarity, how special revelation bears on the whole of their lives, and recalibrate their foundations accordingly, rather than living with a compartmentalized faith that governs Sunday and stays silent everywhere else. Historical controversies (antinomian, neonomian, the Owen/Ball/Petto dispute over Sinai, the Marrow Controversy) are engaged because they are where the historic consensus was forced into precision — not as a source of ammunition. Contemporary departures from that consensus are engaged for the same reason, as a live pressure-test that clarifies the constructive account, not as the reason this project exists.

## Thesis

This project defends, across both Testaments, a strict 1646 Westminster Standards account of the relationship between the covenant of works and the covenant of grace: that Adam stood before God under a covenant of works requiring perfect and personal obedience (WCF 7.2); that upon the fall God was pleased to make a second covenant, the covenant of grace, offering life and salvation freely through Christ (WCF 7.3); and that this one covenant of grace has been administered differently under each Testament — "by promises, prophecies, sacrifices, circumcision, the passover, and other types and ordinances" under the Old (LC 34), and "in the preaching of the word, and the administration of the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's supper" under the New (LC 35) — without ever becoming, at any point in redemptive history, two covenants differing in substance (WCF 7.6).

Everything this project argues is an application, defense, or historical vindication of that single claim: **one covenant of grace, two administrations** — not sameness, not rupture. Moral law, natural law, and grace all have to be located correctly within that frame under each administration, or the frame itself comes apart.

## What is at stake

Getting this wrong is not a narrow technical error; it fails in one of two directions.

Collapse the covenants into simple sameness — treat Sinai as though it differed from the New Testament only in ceremonial furniture, with no real works-shaped element in its own terms — and Hebrews' argument that the first covenant was "faulty" (Heb 8:7) becomes unintelligible, and the antinomian temptation to flatten law and gospel gains ground it should not have.

Separate the covenants into two different economies of salvation — treat Sinai, or the Mosaic administration generally, as governed by a genuinely different way of acceptance with God than the promise made to Abraham — and the result is either a functional Marcionism (the God of Sinai is not quite the God of the gospel) or a two-kingdoms political theology that cuts the moral law loose from Christ's mediatorial reign over all of life, on the premise that the Old Testament's civil and typological order has no abiding claim on how the church, the magistrate, or the Christian conscience should be understood now.

Getting this right constructively is the goal; the sharpest test of whether it's actually been gotten right is **Klinean republication** and its political-theological descendants — the contemporary case where the failure mode described above is live, not hypothetical: the school of thought, following Meredith Kline, in which the Mosaic covenant republishes the covenant of works at the national and typological level in a sense strong enough to ground a hard distinction between a "common" civil kingdom (governed by natural law, common to believer and unbeliever alike) and a "redemptive" spiritual kingdom (governed by the gospel) — with the result that Christ's Lordship over civil life, and the Old Testament's abiding moral-legal witness to that Lordship, is muted or bracketed out. This project does not treat every construal of "republication" as suspect — Owen's own administrative-republication reading of Sinai is judged, on the historical evidence, to be defensible within 1646 orthodoxy, and Series IV will make that case in detail, sourced to primary texts, before any contemporary figure is engaged. The line this project draws is not "republication is illegitimate" but "republication that severs Sinai's typical, national works-principle from the one covenant of grace it served, in a way that relativizes Christ's kingship over civil and moral life, goes beyond what 1646 orthodoxy will bear." Where contemporary authors (Kline, VanDrunen, and others) are engaged, that engagement will follow the standards set in `.claude/agents/contemporary-interlocutor.md`: their strongest stated case represented honestly, with sources, before any critique is offered.

## Confessional anchor

- **WCF 7.1–3** — the covenant of works with Adam, and the covenant of grace made upon the fall.
- **WCF 7.5–6** — one covenant of grace, "differently administered in the time of the law, and in the time of the gospel," yet never "two covenants of grace, differing in substance."
- **LC 31–36** — the covenant of grace made with Christ as the second Adam (Q31); its gracious character (Q32); the fact and manner of its differing administrations (Q33–35); its one Mediator (Q36).
- **LC 91–97** and **WCF 19** — the moral law's continuing use under the gospel, once the ceremonial and judicial law (as a body of Mosaic civil statutes for Israel as a body politic) has expired (WCF 19.3–4) — the confessional ground on which any account of "natural law" or "general equity" in this project's political-implications series must stand or fall.

These citations are checked against primary 1646 text (not later American revisions) as a standing discipline; see `.claude/agents/confessional-reviewer.md`.

## Historical method

The 1646 text itself was drafted, on the evidence of the Assembly's own debates, to leave room for more than one construal of exactly how Sinai relates to the covenant of works and the covenant of grace — Ball's "administration only" reading, Cameron's and Bolton's "subservient covenant," Owen's and Goodwin's "administrative republication," and Petto's "double obedience" formula all sit within the bounds the Confession's language was built to permit; the Assembly's own stated rule was that it inquired "not after names, but things." This project takes that historical diversity seriously rather than flattening it, and Series IV exists specifically to trace it — through the Assembly's floor debates, the Antinomian and Neonomian controversies that pressed on it from both sides, the Marrow Controversy that redeployed it a century later for pastoral rather than polemical ends, and its resurfacing in the Hodge/Murray disagreement, Geerhardus Vos's own moderating reading of the Mosaic covenant (CONFIRMED per Michael Lynch's essay "Vos, Republication, and the Mosaic Covenant," recovered from web archive — see `research/reading-notes/series-iv-historical-debates/lynch-vos-republication-mosaic-covenant.md`; Vos grounds Israel's obedience/blessing connection in a non-meritorious "symbolico-typical sphere of appropriateness of expression," explicitly denying merit while still affirming a real conditional connection — a genuine middle term between Owen-style administrative republication and Kline's harder "substantial" version, not simply a way station en route to Kline), and the modern Klinean revival — before turning to the contemporary critique that is this project's actual point.

## Relation to existing treatments

The current field of serious contemporary covenant-theology treatments already contains strong "pure administration" defenses against Klinean republication: Stephen G. Myers, *God to Us: Covenant Theology in Scripture* (Reformation Heritage, 2021), explicitly names and rejects Kline's law-covenant/promise-covenant distinction as the position's linchpin (CONFIRMED, direct quotation on file); Richard P. Belcher Jr., *The Fulfillment of the Promises of God* (Mentor, 2020), disagrees with Kline's reading of the Mosaic covenant while representing him fairly enough that a sympathetic Klinean reviewer conceded the representation was accurate (CONFIRMED); Ryan McGraw's short primer *What Is Covenant Theology?* (Reformation Heritage, 2013; Crossway, 2024) takes a similar administration-of-grace line at a popular level (WELL-ATTESTED). On the other side, Michael Horton and Harrison Perkins hold a moderated-Klinean position (grace-administration with a real typological works-dimension), and David VanDrunen and J. V. Fesko build the harder Escondido two-kingdoms political theology on Kline's "substantial" republication reading — VanDrunen's *Divine Covenants and Moral Order* (Eerdmans, 2014) is the fullest contemporary statement of the political theology this project is actually answering (WELL-ATTESTED on structure; primary text not yet read in full). The 2016 OPC "Report of the Committee to Study Republication" is a deliberately hedged non-endorsement of any one position, not a resolution (CONFIRMED, fetched directly).

Stephen Wolfe's *The Case for Christian Nationalism* (Canon Press, 2022) already mounts a public critique of VanDrunen specifically (CONFIRMED, direct quotation on file) — but on the eschatology of the Adamic dominion mandate, not on natural law's validity or neutrality; Wolfe is himself a committed natural-law proponent who rejects "R2K" conclusions while retaining its premises. This project's own critique runs on different ground (natural law's actual accessibility and its independence, or lack thereof, from Christ's mediatorial kingship) and should be careful to distinguish its reasons for rejecting Klinean 2K from Wolfe's, per `.claude/agents/contemporary-interlocutor.md` — engaging him as an intramural interlocutor whose critique overlaps in target but not in argument, not folding his conclusions in as if they were this project's own.

A fourth, more directly confessional critique is Zachary Garris's chapter "A Critique of Modern 'Reformed Two Kingdoms'" in *Reformed Christian Politics* (Wolfe, Garris, and Sean McGowan; Berith Press, 2026) — CONFIRMED, extensive direct quotation on file at `research/reading-notes/contemporary-kline-critics/garris-critique-of-modern-r2k.md`. Garris argues primarily from WCF 19.4's general equity and the unity of the Noahic covenant, not from natural law's content or neutrality, and is careful to distinguish "administrative" from "substantial" readings of Kline's own republication position rather than flattening him — a carefulness this project's own language should match.

Two further living voices turn out to be much closer to this project's own natural-law approach than initially credited, and an earlier pass's failure to find them was a real miss, not their absence. **Matt Marino** (reformedclassicalist.com) directly engages Van Til's role in the 1920s Kuyper/Hoeksema common-grace debate and R2K's natural law specifically, including a piece — "'Noetic Effects of Sin' Objections" — aimed at nearly the exact question this project's own Strand 1 is reading toward (CONFIRMED; `research/reading-notes/contemporary-kline-critics/`). **Jonathan Tomes** (postnuance.substack.com) mounts a direct critique of VanDrunen's "spatial" (rather than properly "modal") reading of the two kingdoms, citing David Dickson against him, and independently treats the 1646-vs-1788 American revision as substantive rather than cosmetic — the same confessional discipline this project insists on, arrived at without any apparent coordination with it (CONFIRMED; `research/reading-notes/contemporary-kline-critics/tomes-twofold-kingdom-and-good-works.md`).

Given Marino and Tomes, the honest statement of this project's distinctive contribution is narrower than first drafted here. It is not that no one else is making any part of this argument — Marino, Tomes, Wolfe, and Garris each already occupy a piece of it, in essay or chapter form. It is that no one has yet put the *whole synthesis* in one place: Augustine and Aquinas on the noetic effects of sin, the Reformed scholastic political tradition (Calvin, Junius, Althusius, Turretin), the American Covenanter mediatorial-kingship writers (Symington and McLeod), and Van Til, argued across both Testaments, pinned specifically to 1646 text throughout rather than "the Westminster Standards" loosely, and carried through to pastoral application (Series V) rather than stopping at doctrine or polemic — in one sustained, multi-part treatment. Myers/Belcher/McGraw (covenant-theology angle), Wolfe (eschatology-of-dominion angle), Garris (confessional-general-equity and Noahic-covenant angle), and Marino and Tomes (natural-law/mediatorial-kingship angles, each covering real ground this project also wants to cover) are engaged throughout as living Reformed brethren running genuinely parallel work, per `.claude/agents/contemporary-interlocutor.md` — credited for real convergence wherever it exists, not treated as literature this project aims to surpass. Series IV and V carry the comprehensive synthesis; the material feeding it is currently being read through and reasoned out, with Marino's and Tomes's own work folded directly into that reading alongside the historical sources — see `research/natural-law-synthesis-reading-map.md` for the working hypothesis and open questions, and `research/contemporary-interlocutors-map.md` for the fuller contemporary landscape.

## Scope

- **Series I — Foundations.** The covenant of works and covenant of grace stated and defended from Scripture and the Standards; terms defined (moral law, natural law, administration, substance).
- **Series II — OT Covenants.** Abrahamic, Sinaitic, Davidic administrations of the one covenant of grace; the Owen/Ball/Petto/Cameron question worked out exegetically and confessionally.
- **Series III — NT Continuity.** The New Testament administration as the same covenant "in more fulness, evidence, and efficacy" (LC 35); ecclesiological, sacramental, and church-governmental implications of the change in administration.
- **Series IV — Historical Debates.** The Westminster Assembly's actual debates; the Puritan and Covenanter reception; the Antinomian, Neonomian, and Marrow controversies; the 19th–21st century Hodge/Murray/Kline lineage.
- **Series V — Implications.** Pastoral, civil, and lay-practical consequences — preaching law and gospel, the magistrate and general equity, and ordinary Christian conscience, each treated as a distinct concern rather than collapsed into the others (see `.claude/agents/pastoral-political.md`).

## Non-goals

- **Not fundamentally an anti-Kline, anti-VanDrunen, anti-R2K, or anti-Van-Til project.** It is a constructive reconstruction of the classical Reformed position on natural law, moral law, and their relation to special revelation, for the sake of ordinary Christians recalibrating their own foundations. The debates sharpen that reconstruction; they aren't its point. If an article's critical engagement ever outweighs its constructive statement of the positive doctrine, that's a sign the article has drifted from the project's actual purpose.
- Not a general-audience apologetics site.
- Not a polemical takedown blog — precision in service of clarity, not point-scoring.
- Not relitigating Owen/Ball/Petto as an in-house antiquarian squabble; that precision serves the constructive account first, and only secondarily sharpens the contemporary engagement with Klinean republication and its political-theological uses.
- Not treating Wolfe, Garris, Marino, Tomes, Beers, Lynch, and other living Reformed interlocutors as opponents in the sense Kline's system is treated — see `.claude/agents/contemporary-interlocutor.md`.

## Closing

The reason to get any of this right is not architectural elegance. It is that the church living under the New Testament administration is being told, in her worship, her government, and her assurance, exactly how much of Sinai has passed away and exactly how much of the ancient promise remains — "yea, and how great riches of glory of this mystery" (Col 1:27) — sufficiently, that the whole labor of getting the covenants right terminates, if it is done honestly, in the same place the Larger Catechism itself keeps returning to: Christ, the substance, once shadowed and now exhibited, held forth to all nations in more fulness, evidence, and efficacy than the fathers themselves were given to see.
